जंगली बिल्ली / Jungle Cat

Felis chaus Schreber, 1777 — Felidae

जंगली बिल्ली के नाम में "जंगल" है, पर यह जंगल का नहीं — घास और नरकट का जानवर है। पूर्वी UP में गन्ने के खेत, सरयू के किनारे की नरकट, और झीलों के किनारे की घास — यही इसका घर है। इंसान इसे अक्सर बड़ी घरेलू बिल्ली समझकर नज़रअंदाज़ कर देते हैं — पर यह एक असली जंगली बिल्ली है, शिकारी, संरक्षित, और अपने पारिस्थितिक तंत्र के लिए ज़रूरी।

Quick Identification त्वरित पहचान

FeatureDescription
SizeBody 50–75 cm; tail 20–30 cm; weight 4–8 kg — noticeably larger than domestic cat
ColourSandy-grey to reddish-tawny; unmarked body (no spots on adults)
EarsBlack-tipped with slight tuft — diagnostic
TailRelatively short; black-tipped; faint dark rings
LegsLong, slender — adapted for grassland movement
FaceWhite muzzle; pale around eyes; no distinct facial markings

Confusion with domestic cat: Jungle Cat is significantly larger with long legs, black-tipped ears, and lacks distinct body spots or tabby markings. Its gait is more deliberate and upright. Feral domestic cats in eastern UP are often confused but are smaller, shorter-legged.

Morphology आकारिकी

FeatureMeasurement
Head-body length50–75 cm

Body: Medium-sized wild cat, well-muscled, with relatively long legs — adapted for fast movement through tall grass. Adults lack the obvious body spots seen in young kittens.

Fur: Dense, uniformly sandy-grey to reddish-tawny. Some individuals show faint tabby-like striping on the limbs but body is largely unmarked.

Ears: Relatively tall, black-tipped, with a slight tuft of hair — the most distinctive field character separating it from domestic cats at a glance.

Tail: Shorter than a domestic cat's relative to body size; black-tipped; one or two faint dark rings. The short tail and long legs give a distinctive profile.

Eyes: Yellow-green; pupils contract to narrow vertical slits in daylight. Large relative to face — adapted for low-light hunting.

Ecological Role पारिस्थितिक भूमिका

Grassland mesopredator: Jungle Cat occupies the mesopredator niche in eastern UP grasslands and wetland margins — below the leopard (absent from most of eastern UP) and above the mongoose. It controls:

  • Rodents (primary prey) — rats, mice, hares; particularly important in sugarcane and paddy fields
  • Birds — waterfowl (ducks, coots, moorhens near jhils), ground-nesting birds, pigeons
  • Frogs and toads — seasonal prey near wetlands
  • Snakes — small snakes occasionally taken
  • Large insects — locusts, beetles at times

Sugarcane field ecology: In eastern UP, mature sugarcane fields (October–December at harvest time) form critical habitat for Jungle Cats and for the rodent populations they control. As sugarcane is cut, cats move to adjacent areas. Maintaining scrub and grass buffers around sugarcane fields supports cat populations.

Wetland predator: Felis chaus is a capable swimmer — nicknamed "Reed Cat" and "Swamp Cat" precisely because it actively hunts in reed beds and shallow water margins. It will pursue ducks and coots into water.

Vocalisation: The Jungle Cat's calls are distinct from a domestic cat — a loud "wow" call used in territorial and breeding contexts, heard at night. Kittens use chirping contact calls.

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि एवं मानव उपयोग

Rodent control in sugarcane: The single most important economic service provided by Jungle Cats in eastern UP is rodent control in sugarcane fields. Rodents (particularly rats and bandicoot rats) cause 5–15% sugarcane crop losses annually. A pair of Jungle Cats in or adjacent to a sugarcane field provides significant natural pest control.

Poultry conflict: Jungle Cats occasionally raid poultry — taking free-ranging chickens and ducks near wetlands. This is the primary source of human–jungle cat conflict. Proper poultry housing eliminates most raids.

Legal protection: Schedule II, Wildlife Protection Act 1972. Cannot be killed or captured. Poaching for skin (spotted kitten skins are sometimes sold as leopard cat skins in illegal trade) occurs.

Ancient Egypt: Felis chaus was one of the cats kept by ancient Egyptians alongside the African wildcat (Felis silvestris lybica). Mummified Jungle Cat remains have been found in Egyptian archaeological sites — suggesting this species' association with humans as a grain-store rodent controller predates domestic cat domestication.

Expected Locally स्थानीय रूप से अपेक्षित

अभी तक स्थानीय पुष्टि नहीं। यह विवरण प्रकाशित स्रोतों से है, किसी अवलोकन से नहीं।

Jungle Cat is reported from Terai scrub and grasslands of Balrampur, Bahraich, and Shravasti districts in eastern UP. Possible presence in Basti district near Saryu floodplain reed beds and sugarcane fields — not yet confirmed by systematic survey. Farmers near Saryu and Rapti rivers mention a large "jungli billi" that takes poultry occasionally — likely Jungle Cat. Camera traps in Sohagi Barwa Wildlife Sanctuary (eastern UP) have recorded the species. Most active in twilight and at night — rarely seen clearly.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: One of the most widely distributed wild cats in the world — from Egypt and the Middle East east across Central Asia, through the Indian subcontinent, to Southeast Asia and southern China. Also the westernmost naturally occurring Felidae species after the Eurasian wildcat.

In India: Widespread across the country in grassland, wetland, and scrub habitats. Notably absent from dense closed-canopy forest. Found from the Himalayan foothills to peninsular India.

In Uttar Pradesh: Present throughout the state in suitable habitat — particularly in the Terai grasslands and wetland margins, the Gangetic alluvial floodplain reed beds, and sugarcane-growing belts.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Likely present along Saryu and Rapti floodplain reed beds and in mature sugarcane fields; absence of confirmed records reflects under-survey rather than absence. Most probable occurrence: Terai zone boundary in northern Basti district.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. Felis chaus was formally described by Johann Christian Daniel von Schreber in 1777 in Die Säugthiere in Abbildungen nach der Natur, although the species had been observed earlier by Güldenstädt (1776). Schreber's publication takes nomenclatural priority for the binomial. The species name chaus is derived from the Arabic name for the animal.

Subspecies. Several subspecies have been described across the wide range:

SubspeciesRange
F. c. chausAsia Minor, Middle East, Central Asia
F. c. affinisHimalayan foothills, Nepal, northern India (including UP)
F. c. kutasBengal and Bangladesh
F. c. prateriPakistan, Sindh

Family and order placement. Belongs to the order Carnivora, family Felidae (cats), genus Felis. The genus Felis contains small cats including the domestic cat's ancestor (Felis silvestris lybica). Felis chaus is the largest member of the genus Felis. Some taxonomic authorities formerly placed it in a monotypic genus Chaus but current consensus retains it in Felis.

Phylogenetic notes. Molecular phylogenetics confirms that Felis chaus is sister to the domestic cat and African wildcat clade within the Felinae. The Jungle Cat's association with human-modified environments (grain stores, irrigation systems, cropland margins) suggests a long evolutionary history of exploiting human habitats. Felis chaus × domestic cat hybrids ("Chausie") are documented and indicate relatively recent divergence.

IUCN status rationale. Assessed as Least Concern (LC) by the IUCN Red List (Nowell, 2020). Widespread, but populations have declined over much of the range due to habitat loss (draining of wetlands, destruction of reed beds) and hunting. In India, Schedule II protection provides nominal safeguard.
