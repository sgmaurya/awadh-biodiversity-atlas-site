Red Blister Beetle / Spotted Blister Beetle

Mylabris pustulata Thunberg, 1791 — Meloidae

छाला भृंग — एक ऐसा कीड़ा है जिसे छूने पर छाले पड़ जाते हैं। इसके शरीर में "कैन्थेरिडिन" नामक ज़हरीला रासायनिक होता है, जो त्वचा पर पड़ने पर कुछ घंटों में फफोले बना देता है। इसके लार्वा का जीवन-चक्र भी असाधारण है — वे परजीवी हैं और अपना विकास एकाकी मधुमक्खियों के घोंसलों में करते हैं।

Quick Identification त्वरित पहचान

FeatureDescription
Size15–25 mm; elongated, soft-bodied
ColourBrilliant orange-red elytra (wing covers) with black spots or irregular bands
BodySoft, flexible; head narrower than thorax (characteristic of Meloidae)
WarningAposematic — bright colours warn predators of toxicity
BehaviourAggregated feeding on flowers; releases haemolymph when disturbed (autohemorrhage)

Field tip: Bright orange-red beetle (larger than a ladybird) on soybean or bean flowers in September–October, often in groups. Do not handle with bare hands — the toxic haemolymph causes skin blistering on contact.

Morphology आकारिकी

FeatureMeasurement
Body length15–25 mm
ElytraSoft, flexible; orange-red with 3–6 black spots or transverse bands
HeadBlack, narrower than thorax
AntennaeLong; serrated in males

Head: Black, narrower than the thorax — characteristic of blister beetles (Meloidae). Long, jointed antennae, serrated in males.

Elytra: Soft and flexible (not hardened like most beetles) — deep orange-red to scarlet, with 3–6 irregular black spots or transverse bands. The pattern varies between individuals and populations.

Body: Elongate, soft. Abdomen often shows through the elytra gap. Males have a slightly bent last antennal segment (useful for sex determination).

Defence secretion: When threatened, the beetle reflexively bleeds from leg joints (reflex bleeding / autohemorrhage). The yellowish haemolymph contains cantharidin at concentrations of 0.01–1.0% — sufficient to cause blistering.

Ecological Role पारिस्थितिक भूमिका

Hypermetamorphosis — multiple larval stages:

Blister beetle larvae undergo hypermetamorphosis — one of the most dramatic developmental sequences in the insect world:

  1. Triungulin (first instar): Tiny, active, six-legged larva that hatches from egg. Actively seeks out solitary bee nests (ground-nesting bees like Amegilla, Andrena). It may hitchhike on a bee (phoresis) to reach the nest.
  2. Second instar: Fat, legless, C-shaped larva — settled in bee brood cell, feeding on pollen stores and bee egg.
  3. Third instar (coarctate): Quiescent "pseudo-pupa" — waits through unfavourable conditions.
  4. Fourth instar: Resume feeding; consume remaining pollen.
  5. True pupa: Immobile metamorphosis stage.
  6. Adult: Emerges from ground to feed on flowers.

This progression through 5–6 morphologically distinct stages is unique to Meloidae and a few related families.

Adult diet: Flower feeding — primarily Leguminosae (soybean, bean, groundnut) and Asteraceae. Aggregated feeding on flowers by many adults simultaneously can cause significant defoliation.

Cantharidin ecology: Cantharidin deters most predators. However, some specialist predators (certain beetles, birds like starlings) consume blister beetles without harm. The compound is passed through the food chain — predators can accumulate sub-lethal doses.

Life Cycle / Phenology जीवन चक्र

MonthEvent
Mar–MayEggs laid in soil by overwintering female (sometimes)
Jun–AugTriungulins active; seek solitary bee nests during monsoon
Aug–OctLarval development in bee nests underground
Sep–OctAdult emergence — peak activity on soybean/bean flowers
NovAdults die after mating and egg-laying; pupae overwinter

Egg laying: Female deposits 200–400 eggs in clusters in the soil. Triungulins hatch and must locate a solitary bee nest — those that fail die. Only a tiny fraction survive to adulthood — hence the large egg numbers.

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि एवं मानव उपयोग

Crop damage: Adults aggregate on soybean, bean (Phaseolus), groundnut, and cotton flowers — eating petals, pollen, and sometimes leaves. Heavy aggregations (50–200 beetles per plant) can cause significant pod set reduction in soybean. Outbreaks occur particularly in warm, wet post-monsoon conditions.

Cantharidin — toxicology:

  • Cantharidin is a potent toxin causing genitourinary irritation, skin blistering, kidney damage
  • "Spanish Fly" (Lytta vesicatoria, a related European blister beetle) was historically used as an alleged aphrodisiac — actually causes cantharidin toxicosis; historically used as a murder weapon
  • Handling warning: Never touch blister beetles with bare hands; if haemolymph contacts skin, wash immediately with soap and water; blistering may still occur 2–4 hours later

Cantharidin — medical applications:

  • Wart removal: pharmaceutical-grade cantharidin applied topically destroys wart tissue by causing controlled blistering — effective dermatological treatment
  • Anticancer research: cantharidin inhibits mitosis and induces apoptosis in cancer cell lines — active research area (especially hepatocellular carcinoma)
  • Traditional medicine in China: "Ban Mao" (dried blister beetles) used in TCM — controlled dosing for skin conditions; toxic if misused

Livestock hazard: If consumed by horses or cattle in hay/pasture, cantharidin causes severe acute toxicosis — kidney and bladder damage, internal blistering, potentially fatal. Cattle deaths from blister beetle contamination in hay are documented.

अभी तक स्थानीय पुष्टि नहीं। यह विवरण प्रकाशित स्रोतों से है, किसी अवलोकन से नहीं।

Aggregations of 10–100 beetles have been observed on soybean flowers in Basti district in September–October. Farmers notice the orange-red beetles but many are unaware of the blistering hazard — hand weeding in affected fields causes skin blistering. The local name khajua kira (itch-causing insect) reflects the blistering effect known to farmers. After heavy monsoons, outbreaks are more likely. Birds (particularly Asian Pied Starling, BRD-0012) have been observed attempting to eat blister beetles, then abandoning them — apparent toxicity recognition.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Widespread across South Asia, Southeast Asia, and parts of Africa. Found from India through Sri Lanka, Pakistan, and onwards to the eastern edge of its range.

In India: Distributed throughout the Indian subcontinent in agricultural and semi-arid zones; very common in crop belts of central and northern India.

In Uttar Pradesh: Found in all agricultural districts, with peak activity in legume-growing areas. Particularly common in kharif (monsoon-season) crop zones.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Occurs on soybean, bean, and groundnut fields. Post-monsoon aggregations on flowers are the most visible occurrence; locally known as khajua kira.

Range notes: Distribution is closely linked to the presence of flowering legumes and the solitary bee populations that serve as larval hosts.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. Mylabris pustulata was described by Carl Peter Thunberg in 1791 in Dissertatio Entomologica, Uppsala. The species was originally placed in the genus Mylabris and has remained there without significant revision. The genus Mylabris was established by Fabricius in 1775 and now comprises several hundred species worldwide.

Subspecies. No subspecies are currently recognised for Mylabris pustulata; it is treated as monotypic. However, considerable variation in spot pattern and number is observed across its range and may represent geographic population differentiation not yet formalised.

Family and order placement. Belongs to the order Coleoptera (beetles), family Meloidae (blister beetles), subfamily Meloinae. The family Meloidae is notable for its production of cantharidin and for the unusual hypermetamorphosis of its larvae. The genus Mylabris is the largest genus in Meloidae, containing over 250 described species primarily distributed in Asia and Africa.

Phylogenetic notes. Molecular studies of Meloidae place Mylabris within a clade of Old World blister beetles with African and Asian radiations. The hypermetamorphosis life cycle is ancestral to the entire family, shared with North American genera such as Epicauta and Meloe. Cantharidin production is also a synapomorphy (shared derived character) of the entire family.

IUCN status rationale. This species has not been formally assessed by the IUCN Red List. As a widely distributed agricultural insect present across much of South and Southeast Asia, it is not considered of conservation concern. Populations fluctuate with rainfall patterns and host bee availability.
