भूमि जूँ / Forest Leaf Woodlouse

Burmoniscus javanensis (Searle, 1922) — Philosciidae

भूमि जूँ — बगीचे की गीली मिट्टी, पत्ती-कूड़े, और पुरानी लकड़ी के नीचे तेज़ी से दौड़ने वाला छोटा भूरा जीव। सात जोड़ी टाँगें, चपटा-अंडाकार बदन, भूरे-जैतूनी रंग में धब्बेदार छलावरण। यह कीड़ा नहीं है — क्रस्टेशियन है, केकड़े-झींगे का ज़मीनी रिश्तेदार। गोली जैसा बॉल नहीं बनाता — बस तेज़ी से भाग जाता है। सड़े पत्ते और फफूँद खाकर मिट्टी की उर्वरता बढ़ाता है।

Quick Identification त्वरित पहचान

FeatureDescription
Size8–12 mm length; 4–5 mm width — smaller than a fingernail
BodyOval-elongated; 7 visible thoracic segments (pereonites); slightly flattened
ColourMottled grey-brown to olive-brown with pale yellowish lateral patches
Legs7 pairs; visible from above; slender and relatively long
MovementFast — dashes immediately when uncovered; much faster than European pillbugs
DefenceCannot roll into a ball; relies on speed and camouflage
HabitatUnder damp leaf litter, loose bark, and garden mulch

Field tip: Lift any damp leaf or loose bark in a Basti garden — if a small oval creature runs away quickly without rolling into a ball, it is a woodlouse. Unlike the introduced European pillbug, this native species relies on speed for escape. The mottled colouration provides excellent camouflage on leaf litter. Count 7 leg pairs (insects have 3) to confirm it is not an insect.

Morphology आकारिकी

FeatureMeasurement
Body length8–12 mm

AntennaeOuter pair 13-segmented; inner pair vestigial

Body: Oval-elongated; slightly flattened dorsally; 7 pereonite (thoracic) segments plus a narrower pleosome and pleotelson.

Colour: Mottled grey-brown to olive-brown; pale lateral patches giving a dappled appearance; ventral surface paler cream-grey.

Uropods: Short paired appendages at the tail end; visible from above; used for identification to family level.

Eyes: Sessile compound eyes; small but distinct; on lateral head margin.

Legs: 7 pairs; relatively long and slender — adapted for speed over armour.

Defence: No conglobation (ball-rolling) ability; relies on speed, crypsis, and thigmotaxis (pressing against substrate) for defence.

Ecological Role पारिस्थितिक भूमिका

Native terrestrial crustacean — Indo-Gangetic detritivore. Burmoniscus javanensis is among the most ecologically abundant native terrestrial isopods in the leaf litter of gardens, orchards, and managed landscapes of eastern UP. As a native species, it shreds dead leaf material into fragments available for microbial decomposition.

Leaf litter processing: Woodlice feed primarily on partially-decomposed leaves, consuming fungal hyphae, bacteria, and soft plant tissue together. This physical fragmentation creates fine particulate organic matter (FPOM) that dramatically increases the surface area available for decomposer microbes — accelerating nutrient release into the soil.

Calcium cycling — specialist function: Like all terrestrial isopods, Burmoniscus has a two-phase moult (anterior half first, then posterior). During moulting, it consumes its own shed cuticle to recapture calcium — a critical function in calcium-poor soils. Their carbonate-rich bodies become a calcium deposit when they die, improving local soil chemistry.

Coprophage and recycler: Woodlice consume their own faecal pellets (coprophagy) — extracting maximum nutrition from food material. Their frass is exceptionally nutrient-dense, making it a highly efficient input to the soil organic layer.

Juvenile (mancae): Hatch with 6 leg pairs; 7th pair added at first moult; otherwise identical to adults. Maturity reached in 2–3 months; multiple broods per year. Lifespan: 1–3 years.

Two-phase moult: Posterior half shed first (woodlouse appears bicoloured during transition); anterior half shed 2–3 days later.

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि एवं मानव उपयोग

Native decomposer — beneficial soil organism: Performs the same ecological service as earthworms and millipedes in the moist leaf litter layer of gardens and orchards — the site of most nutrient mineralisation.

Minor pest of nursery beds: In damp nursery conditions with high organic mulch, populations can become dense enough to damage emerging seedlings by nibbling on cotyledons and roots. Usually controlled by reducing mulch thickness and improving drainage rather than pesticide application.

Soil health proxy: Woodlouse populations are highly sensitive to soil acidification, desiccation, and pesticide accumulation. A garden that maintains a visible woodlouse population under its mulch and leaf litter is maintaining functional decomposer ecology.

Taxonomic gap: The terrestrial isopod fauna of the Indo-Gangetic plain is poorly documented — eastern UP is a taxonomic blank for this group even at the species inventory level. A basic survey from Basti district would be a genuine scientific contribution.

Found year-round under any damp, organic-rich surface in Basti district — leaf litter in mango orchards, under loose bark of Ficus trees, beneath damp newspaper or jute bags in storage areas, and in vegetable garden mulch. Moves quickly when exposed — the speed and mottled brown colour make it harder to spot. Density is highest in shaded, continuously moist spots. Not identified to species by local residents — usually dismissed as keeda (bug) or confused with other small crawling arthropods. A careful look at the segmented plated body and 7 visible leg pairs distinguishes it from any insect.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Widespread in tropical South and Southeast Asia — India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, and the Malay Archipelago (including Java, from which the species name is derived). One of the most commonly encountered terrestrial isopods in humid tropical landscapes.

In India: Distributed across tropical and subtropical zones — peninsular India and the northern plains. Distribution data sparse due to neglect of this taxonomic group in Indian zoological literature.

In Uttar Pradesh: Present in garden, orchard, and forest-margin leaf litter across the state. Poorly documented due to taxonomic neglect.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Year-round resident; most active July–November. Found in any moist shaded microhabitat with decomposing organic matter.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. Burmoniscus javanensis was originally described as Philoscia javanensis by H.R. Searle in 1922. It was later transferred to the genus Burmoniscus Verhoeff, 1930. The genus name Burmoniscus references Burma (now Myanmar) — one of the geographic centres of diversity for this genus. The species epithet javanensis refers to Java (Indonesia), from which the type specimen was collected.

Subspecies. No subspecies are formally recognised; Burmoniscus javanensis is treated as monotypic.

Family and order placement. Belongs to the order Isopoda, family Philosciidae. The order Isopoda contains approximately 10,000 species worldwide — most are marine or freshwater, but the suborder Oniscidea contains approximately 3,500 species of fully terrestrial isopods (woodlice/pillbugs). The family Philosciidae contains approximately 50 genera of tropical terrestrial isopods, distributed primarily across Asia, Africa, and the Pacific. The genus Burmoniscus contains approximately 15 species in tropical Asia.

Phylogenetic notes. Terrestrial isopods (Oniscidea) evolved from marine ancestors multiple times — the multiple independent colonisations of land by isopods represent one of the most remarkable evolutionary transitions in arthropod history. Unlike insects, which solved the terrestrial problem through exoskeletal waterproofing, woodlice remain physiologically reliant on humidity — explaining their restriction to moist microhabitats. Burmoniscus javanensis represents a lineage that has adapted to tropical humid environments rather than the drier habitats tolerated by the introduced European Armadillidium.

IUCN status rationale. Burmoniscus javanensis has not been assessed by the IUCN Red List. As a widespread, humid-habitat detritivore across tropical Asia, it is not of conservation concern globally. Local populations may be affected by habitat desiccation and pesticide accumulation.
