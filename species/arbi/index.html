अरबी / Taro

Colocasia esculenta (L.) Schott — Araceae

अरबी (घुइयाँ, सारान) — पूरे भारत में, विशेषकर पूर्वी उत्तर प्रदेश में, पानी भरी और गीली जमीन पर उगने वाली एक महत्वपूर्ण फसल। इसका भूमिगत कंद (corm) बिल्कुल अदरक की तरह होता है पर बहुत बड़ा, और इसी को खाते हैं। पत्तियाँ बहुत बड़ी, दिल के आकार की, लंबे डंठल पर होती हैं। पूर्वी उत्तर प्रदेश में इसे सब्जी के रूप में पकाया जाता है और बिहार में अरबी के पत्तों की भी सब्जी बनाई जाती है। कंद में स्टार्च बहुत होता है और यह आसानी से पचने वाली सब्जी है। पानी के खेतों (वेटलैंड्स) में यह पारंपरिक फसल है।

Field tip: Look for fields with large heart-shaped leaves in July–August in waterlogged areas. The plant is tallest in monsoon. Corms are dug from November onwards; the white flesh inside is distinctive.

हिंदी — कैसे पहचानें: बहुत बड़ी दिल जैसी पत्तियाँ, पानी भरे खेतों में, लंबे डंठल वाली। जमीन में सफेद, स्टार्च वाला कंद।

Morphology आकारिकी

Biometrics (cultivated taro, eastern India):

MeasurementRange
Plant height1–2 m
Leaf length30–100 cm
Leaf width20–70 cm
Petiole length30–80 cm
Corm diameter10–30 cm
Corm weight0.5–3 kg

Root system: Fibrous roots from the basal plate of the corm; shallow but extensive.

Corm: Underground, roughly globular or slightly flattened, brown outer skin (periderm), white, cream, or purple flesh inside depending on cultivar. Contains abundant starch.

Leaves: Very large, simple, sagittate or hastate (arrow-shaped with basal lobes), 30–100 cm long, with a distinctive silvery or glaucous waxy coating. Petioles (leaf stalks) are attached at the base of the leaf blade (peltate insertion), making them distinctly different from most vegetables. Veins parallel, prominent.

Inflorescence: A spadix (club-like flower structure) enclosed in a pale yellow or cream spathe. Tiny flowers on the spadix, rarely develop fruit in cultivation.

Offshoots (cormels): Small corms form around the main corm and can be separated for propagation.

हिंदी: बहुत बड़ी पत्तियाँ, दिल जैसी, चाँदी जैसी परत। डंठल बहुत लंबा। जमीन में कंद, सफेद गूदा, स्टार्च वाला।

Behaviour & Ecology व्यवहार और पारिस्थितिकी

A tropical herbaceous perennial native to South and Southeast Asia, cultivated worldwide for its starchy corms. In the Basti plains, it is a traditional monsoon crop, thriving in waterlogged and wetland areas. The plant requires high moisture, warm temperatures, and grows through the monsoon season. Growth is rapid once established; a single corm produces a plant with multiple leaves in 3–4 months.

The plant is shade-tolerant and often intercropped in rice paddies or grown on field margins near water. Although rarely producing flowers and seeds, the plant reproduces vegetatively from cormels (small underground buds).

हिंदी: यह एशियाई पौधा है; पानी में अच्छा उगता है। गर्मी और बारिश चाहिए। धीमी गति से बढ़ता है पर पानी के खेतों के लिए आदर्श।

Associated Species / Interactions संबंधित प्रजातियाँ

  • Field companions: Often grown in or near rice paddies; intercropped with aquatic plants.
  • Soil benefit: Deep roots do not compete with shallow-rooted rice; improves field utilization.

Pests and Diseases कीट और रोग

  • Leaf spots (fungal): various fungal pathogens; worse in humid conditions; improve with better air flow.
  • Root rot (fungal): from poor drainage or waterlogging; ensure adequate (but not excessive) water management.
  • Insects: Rarely serious; occasional caterpillars on leaves.
  • Generally hardy: Taro is one of the most pest-hardy crops; disease-free seed corms are the main prevention.

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि प्रासंगिकता

Food use:

The corm is the primary edible part, boiled or roasted as a vegetable, mashed, or made into flour. The leaves (in some regions, especially Bengal and Bihar) are cooked as a green vegetable. The petioles are also sometimes used.

Nutritional value: Taro corms are rich in starch, with moderate protein and fiber. They are easily digestible and suitable for infants and elderly persons. The corm contains calcium and phosphorus.

Cultivation advantages: Taro is a traditional and reliable monsoon crop in wetland areas of eastern UP. It requires minimal inputs beyond land preparation and water management. A single corm planted in May produces 2–10 cormels (offshoots) harvestable in November–January, providing both main corm and seed material for next season.

Varieties: Landrace varieties are common, differing in corm size and flesh color (white, cream, or purple). Colocasia antiquorum (giant taro) is sometimes grown separately; the form grown in Basti is primarily Colocasia esculenta (Dasheen type).

Yield: High under good management on moist soils; a home plot gives a family its corms for the season.

Importance: Taro is a staple starch crop in traditional South Asian agriculture, providing food security in monsoon and post-monsoon seasons. The crop is particularly important in areas where rice is also grown, as taro can be integrated into wetland farming systems.

हिंदी: पानी के खेतों में परंपरागत फसल। कंद स्टार्च भरा, आसानी से पचने वाली सब्जी। पत्तियाँ भी खा सकते हैं। बीज के कंद बचाते हैं अगली फसल के लिए।

Expected in wetland and waterlogged areas of Basti district, particularly in rice fields and field margins from June to October when the plant is visibly growing. Less common than in deeper eastern regions (like Bihar), but present in suitable moist habitats. The large distinctive leaves in July–September are the easiest identification mark.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Native to South and Southeast Asia (India and perhaps the Philippines); now cultivated in all tropical regions.

In India: Grown throughout the tropics and subtropics, particularly in wetland regions. Especially important in eastern and southern India.

In Uttar Pradesh: Present in all wetland areas, especially in eastern districts.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Traditional crop in waterlogged and wetland areas; less common than in deeper eastern UP.

Field rule: A very large-leaved herbaceous plant with heart-shaped glaucous leaves on long petioles, in waterlogged areas, with an underground starchy white corm is taro.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. Carl Linnaeus described this species as Arum esculentum in 1753 in Species Plantarum. Transferred to Colocasia by Heinrich Schott in 1832. The genus Colocasia is derived from a Sanskrit word meaning "peppercorn" (a plant of similar form). The species name esculenta means "edible."

Subspecies. Monotypic — though two major cultivar groups are recognized (Dasheen, from South Asia, with a single large corm; and Eddoe, with multiple smaller cormels).

Family and order placement. Araceae (arum family), order Alismatales. The family also includes Xanthosoma, Alocasia, and Amorphophallus.

Phylogenetic notes. Colocasia esculenta is the most economically important genus in the Araceae; DNA evidence suggests multiple independent domestications.

IUCN status rationale. Evaluated as Least Concern (LC). Widespread in cultivation and traditional agriculture; stable across its range.
