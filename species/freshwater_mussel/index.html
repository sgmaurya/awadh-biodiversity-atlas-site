सीप / Freshwater Mussel

Lamellidens marginalis (Lamarck, 1819) — Unionidae

सीप — घाघरा और राप्ती की रेतीली-मिट्टी तलहटी में आधी धँसी बड़ी काली-भूरी सीप। बाहर से खुरदरी और काली, अंदर से चमकदार मोती जैसी सफेद। हर घंटे दो-पाँच लीटर पानी छानती है — नदी की प्राकृतिक सफाई करने वाली। इसके लार्वा (ग्लोकिडिया) रोहू और कटला की गिल्स पर परजीवी बनकर पलते हैं — मछली और सीप का अद्भुत जैविक रिश्ता।

Field tip: Empty shells on Ghaghara sand bars (best February–May) are the easiest sign. The interior nacre is bright white-silver — impossible to mistake for any rock. Living mussels are harder to see: look in clear, slow water 30–100 cm deep over sandy substrate. The shell is always partly buried in sediment with only the siphon end exposed.

Morphology आकारिकी

FeatureMeasurement
Shell length8–14 cm
Shell heightup to 7 cm
Weight80–200 g (shell + body)

Shell: Elongate-oval; solid and heavy; umbo (beak) near the front end; outer surface with concentric growth rings indicating age. Exterior greenish-brown to dark olive-black; interior bright silvery-white iridescent nacre.

Hinge: Pseudocardinal and lateral teeth; two adductor muscles hold valves shut with enormous force.

Foot: Large, muscular, tongue-shaped; used for slow locomotion through soft substrate and burrowing.

Mantle: Thin tissue lining inner shell; secretes nacre and forms glochidia (larval stage).

Siphons: Inhalant and exhalant siphons extend slightly from shell for water circulation when feeding.

Ecological Role पारिस्थितिक भूमिका

Water filtration — the living biofilter: A single adult Lamellidens marginalis filters 2–5 litres of water per hour, removing suspended particles, phytoplankton, bacteria, and organic matter. Dense mussel beds act as the kidneys of a river system, maintaining water clarity and reducing algal blooms. In village ponds and slow river stretches, mussel populations are a critical but overlooked water quality regulator.

Nutrient cycling: Mussels package suspended organic particles into compact faecal pellets that sink and enrich bottom sediments — transferring nutrients from the water column to the sediment food web, feeding worms, insect larvae, and decomposers.

Host for fish larvae — glochidial parasitism: The larval stage (glochidium) of Lamellidens is an obligate parasite on fish gills and fins. Female mussels release clouds of glochidia that attach to passing fish (especially cyprinids — Rohu, Catla), encyst in gill tissue, and transform into juvenile mussels before dropping to the riverbed. This remarkable lifecycle links the mussel entirely to the health of the fish population.

Calcium sink: Mussel shells concentrate calcium carbonate, locking it out of the water column. Shell deposits after death form hard-bottom habitat used by caddisfly larvae, snails, and invertebrates.

Filter feeding: Phytoplankton (diatoms, green algae, cyanobacteria), bacteria and dissolved organic matter, detritus particles, zooplankton nauplii and protozoans.

Lifespan: 10–15 years; growth rings on shell are approximately annual. Females brood glochidia in the gill chambers (marsupia) through the monsoon season.

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि एवं मानव उपयोग

Freshwater pearl culture: Lamellidens marginalis has been used in traditional and experimental freshwater pearl cultivation — a nucleus bead is implanted in the mantle and the mussel coats it with nacre. The Central Inland Fisheries Research Institute (CIFRI) has promoted small-scale pearl culture from this species in eastern India.

Food source: Eaten by fishing communities (mallah, nishad) along Ghaghara and Rapti rivers — boiled or roasted. Seasonal harvest, particularly during post-monsoon low-water periods when mussels are accessible.

Shell craft: Mother-of-pearl (nacre) inner surface used for decorative inlay in small craft items. Empty shells historically used as bowls, scrapers, and lime sources (burnt for calcium oxide).

Water quality indicator: Mussel populations decline rapidly with silt loading, pesticide runoff, and organic pollution. Their presence in a river reach is a strong indicator of reasonable water quality; disappearance signals degradation.

Threat from sand mining: Intense sand extraction from Ghaghara and Rapti riverbeds destroys mussel beds by removing the stable substrate. This is currently the most significant local threat.

Empty shells are commonly found along Ghaghara riverbanks near Basti, particularly on sand bars exposed during the dry season (February–May). Living populations are in deeper water (30–100 cm) with sandy-muddy substrate; best observed at low-water margins or by wading in clear river stretches. Fishing communities along the Ghaghara are aware of the species — it is part of local ecological knowledge even if rarely discussed with outsiders. Shell middens at otter latrine sites along the Ghaghara riverbank are indirect evidence of mussel populations.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Indian subcontinent — found throughout the major river systems of India, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Sri Lanka. Also recorded in Myanmar. One of the most widespread freshwater bivalves of South Asia.

In India: Distributed across all major river systems — the Ganga plain, Brahmaputra basin, peninsular river systems. Particularly abundant in the slower reaches of large alluvial rivers.

In Uttar Pradesh: Present throughout the state in major rivers (Ganga, Yamuna, Ghaghara, Rapti, Gomti) and associated oxbow lakes and large ponds.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Present in sandy-muddy substrate reaches of the Ghaghara, Rapti, and Kuwano rivers; also in large undisturbed village ponds. Most accessible at low-water (February–May) when river sand bars are exposed.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. Lamellidens marginalis was described by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck in 1819 as Unio marginalis in Histoire Naturelle des Animaux sans Vertèbres, Vol. 6. The genus Lamellidens was established by Simpson in 1900. The genus name refers to the lamellar (plate-like) teeth of the hinge (lamell- = plate, dens = tooth). The species epithet marginalis refers to the marginal pattern on the shell.

Subspecies. No subspecies are currently formally recognised; Lamellidens marginalis is treated as monotypic. However, considerable variation in shell shape and size across its range has led to some local names and provisional forms that are not given formal taxonomic status.

Family and order placement. Belongs to the order Unionida, family Unionidae (freshwater mussels). The family Unionidae contains approximately 700 species of freshwater bivalves, making it the most species-rich family of freshwater mollusks. The genus Lamellidens contains approximately 8 species, all restricted to the Indian subcontinent and adjacent areas. The order Unionida is notable for the obligate fish-parasitism of the larval stage (glochidia) — a highly specialised reproductive strategy unique to this group.

Phylogenetic notes. Molecular phylogenetics confirms Lamellidens belongs within the Unionidae sensu stricto (true freshwater mussels), closely related to other Asian freshwater mussel genera such as Parreysia and Radiatula. The glochidial parasitism on fish is ancestral in Unionida and is thought to have co-evolved with their fish hosts over millions of years. This tight ecological coupling explains why Unionida species are highly sensitive to changes in fish community composition.

IUCN status rationale. Lamellidens marginalis has not been formally assessed by the IUCN Red List. However, many Unionida species worldwide are among the most threatened freshwater animals, as they are sensitive to water quality degradation, silt loading, flow regulation, and fish host loss. Local population monitoring in Indian rivers is recommended.
