Mound-building Termite / Indian Termite

Odontotermes obesus (Rambur, 1842) — Termitidae

दीमक — पूर्वी UP के हर दीमक के टीले के पीछे यही प्रजाति है। और यही वह प्रजाति है जो ज़मीन के नीचे Termitomyces कवक की खेती करती है — जिसके फल मानसून की पहली बारिश के बाद टीले के पास उगते हैं और जिन्हें गाँव के लोग दीमक छत्ता कहकर खाते हैं। दीमक केवल फसल का दुश्मन नहीं है — यह क्षेत्र के सबसे महत्वपूर्ण पारिस्थितिक इंजीनियरों में से एक है।

Field rule for termite vs ant: Termites have straight bead-like antennae, broad waist, and (for alates) four equal wings. Ants have elbowed antennae, narrow waist, and unequal wings.

The mound: 30 cm to over 1 m tall in O. obesus; conical to chimney-shaped, made of soil mixed with termite saliva; hard outer crust; complex internal architecture with ventilation chimneys, fungus chambers, brood chambers, royal cell. Active mounds maintain stable internal temperature (~28–30°C) and high humidity year-round. A single mound can persist for 20+ years.

The Fungus Garden — The Hidden Mutualism छिपी हुई पारस्परिकता

Inside the underground mound chambers, O. obesus maintains fungus gardens — masses of partially digested plant material inoculated with the white fungus Termitomyces. The fungus does what the termites cannot — it breaks down lignin and cellulose into nutrients the termites can absorb.

The arrangement is fully mutualistic:

  • Termites bring plant material into the mound, chew it, deposit it on the fungus comb
  • Workers eat fungus-degraded comb and feed nestmates
  • The fungus produces small white nodules (conidia) that the termites also eat
  • The fungus cannot survive outside termite mounds — it gets continuous fresh substrate, climate-controlled chambers, and protection from competing fungi

The mushroom: After the first heavy monsoon rain, Termitomyces sends up its fruiting body — the दीमक छत्ता (T. heimii, FNG-0001) or T. clypeatus (FNG-0002). The pseudorrhiza ("false root") of the mushroom runs underground 30–60 cm and connects directly to a termite mound's fungus chamber.

Evolutionary depth: The Macrotermitinae + Termitomyces mutualism is approximately 30 million years old — one of only three independent evolutions of agriculture in animals (alongside leaf-cutter ants and some ambrosia beetles). The relationship is obligate on both sides.

Ecological Role पारिस्थितिक भूमिका

  • Cellulose recycler: One of the few organisms that can efficiently digest lignin and cellulose. Dead wood, dry grass, dung, and leaf litter are returned to the soil as nutrients.
  • Ecosystem engineer: Mound soil is significantly more fertile than surrounding soil (higher clay, organic matter, calcium, and potassium). Trees and shrubs near mounds often grow better.
  • Soil aerator: Tunneling systems extend 30+ m horizontally and several metres deep — aerate soil, improve water infiltration, counteract compaction.
  • Major prey base: Termite alates after monsoon are a critical seasonal food for many species — Black Drongo (BRD-0005), Indian Pond Heron (BRD-0004), Garden Lizard (REP-0001), Common Toad (AMP-0001), bats, frogs, swifts, ants.
  • Fungus partnership: Cultivates Termitomyces heimii (FNG-0001) and T. clypeatus (FNG-0002) — without these termites, these fungi cannot exist.

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि एवं मानव उपयोग

Real conflicts:

  • Live crop damage — sugarcane, wheat seedlings, and groundnut attacked during dry weather
  • Building damage — wooden beams, fence posts, library books in older houses
  • Mound on cropland — reduces arable area marginally

Beneficial roles often overlooked:

  • Soil fertility — fields with active termite populations show measurably higher fertility and water infiltration
  • Composting acceleration — termites accelerate breakdown of post-harvest crop residues
  • Edible alates — collected and roasted as high-protein food by some communities
  • Edible mushrooms — Termitomyces species (FNG-0001, FNG-0002) are entirely termite products
  • Ecological note: The Hindi name सफेद चींटी (safed chinti — "white ant") is a misnomer — termites are NOT ants. They belong to the order Blattodea, the same order as cockroaches.

Management: Target the queen and royal chamber, not foraging workers (killing workers does not eliminate the colony). Bait systems using cellulose impregnated with slow-acting toxin are more effective and ecologically lighter than broad-spectrum soil insecticides.

Active mound-building termite colonies are ubiquitous across Basti district. Mounds of 60–90 cm height are common on field bunds and village wasteland margins. Alate emergence swarms are annual events in July–August coinciding with the first heavy monsoon rains — large swarms around lights at dusk are predictable within 12 hours of significant rainfall. All major predators converge on these events; one documented observation counted 17 Black Drongos, 8 Pond Herons, 3 Common Toads, and multiple geckos feeding at a single swarm over 30 minutes.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Indian subcontinent — found across India, Pakistan, Nepal, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka. One of the most widespread and ecologically significant termite species of South Asia.

In India: Distributed across the entire subcontinent in all warm-season zones. Particularly abundant in agricultural and scrubland habitats of the Gangetic plain and Deccan.

In Uttar Pradesh: Distributed across the entire state wherever suitable substrate exists. Mounds are visible landmarks in every UP district.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Very common year-round. Active mounds visible in field margins throughout the district. Alate swarms every monsoon (June–September).

Research Questions शोध प्रश्न

  1. Where are the active termite mounds in your village? — Map every mound; measure height; note whether termite mushrooms appeared this monsoon.
  2. When does the first alate flight happen each year? — Record date and rainfall; builds a phenology + climate dataset.
  3. Which animals come to feed at alate swarms? — Watch an active swarm at dusk for 30 minutes and record every bird, lizard, frog, and bat.
  4. Do crops grown near termite mounds yield more or less? — Compare yield in mound-proximal vs mound-distal field strips.
  5. What proportion of villagers know that termite mushrooms come from termite mounds? — Survey awareness of the mutualism.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. Odontotermes obesus was described by Pierre Rambur in 1842 in Histoire naturelle des insectes: Névroptères, Paris. The genus name Odontotermes combines the Greek odonto (tooth) and Termes (the termite genus) — referring to the conspicuously toothed mandibles of the soldier caste. The species name obesus means "fat" or "stout" in Latin — referring to the large, rounded body of the worker caste.

Subspecies. No subspecies are formally recognised; Odontotermes obesus is treated as monotypic across its Indian range.

Family and order placement. Belongs to the order Blattodea (which also includes cockroaches), family Termitidae. The placement of termites within Blattodea — rather than the former order Isoptera — is now strongly supported by molecular phylogenetics (Inward et al. 2007). The family Termitidae is the largest termite family (~2,000 species worldwide) and includes all the fungus-growing species (subfamily Macrotermitinae). The genus Odontotermes contains approximately 120 species, all in the Old World tropics.

Phylogenetic notes. Termites are deeply embedded within the order Blattodea as the sister group to the cockroach genus Cryptocercus. The transition from cockroach to termite involved the evolution of eusociality from subsocial wood-feeding behaviour. The fungus-farming behaviour of Macrotermitinae evolved approximately 30 million years ago in Africa and spread to Asia — making Odontotermes one of the world's oldest agricultural lineages.

IUCN status rationale. Odontotermes obesus has not been assessed by the IUCN Red List. As a widespread, ecologically abundant species associated with human-modified and natural landscapes across South Asia, it is not of conservation concern. However, heavy pesticide use in agricultural soils may reduce local colony densities.
