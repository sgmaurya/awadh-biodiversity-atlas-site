गिद्ध / White-rumped Vulture

Gyps bengalensis (Gmelin, 1788) — Accipitridae

गिद्ध — एक समय पूरे भारत में सर्वाधिक पाई जाने वाली सबसे बड़ी गिद्ध, जिसका रंग गहरा भूरा और कमर पर सफ़ेद पट्टी होती थी। यह अब लगभग विलुप्त हो गई है और केवल कुछ स्थानों पर ही दिख पाती है। इसकी कमी से पर्यावरण को बहुत नुक्सान हुआ है क्योंकि मरे पशु सड़ने लगते हैं।

Field tip: The largest and darkest vulture in the region, with the distinctive white rump patch being the key identifying feature. The combination of dark brown body with white rump is unmistakable. Formerly very common, now rare. If a vulture is seen with a white rump patch, photograph it and report immediately, as this species is Critically Endangered.

Size: 75–80 cm long; wingspan 190–210 cm. Weight 4.0–6.0 kg. One of the world's heaviest vultures.

Plumage: Body dark chocolate-brown or sepia-brown. Lower back and rump white, strongly contrasting with brown body—the diagnostic field mark. Upper tail coverts white; tail itself dark brown. Underwing coverts brown; flight feathers dark. Immatures duller overall with reduced white on rump.

Head and neck: Whitish to cream, sparsely feathered (adaptation for feeding on carcasses). Skin of head yellowish.

Eyes: Dark brown; smaller than in some other vultures.

Bill: Very large, strong, hooked, yellowish with dark tip. Specialized for tearing through tough hide.

Legs and feet: Yellow; talons large and curved.

Wings: Massive and broad; when soaring, wings held flat or in slight dihedral. Primaries show marked contrast in flight.

Sexes: Females slightly larger; plumage similar.

Juveniles: Much duller; rump patch reduced or absent; becoming whiter with age.

Vocalisations. Largely silent, as with other vultures:

  1. Hissing and croaking: At carcass or when competing for food.
  2. Wing noise: Loud flapping when taking off or landing.
हिंदी: ज़्यादातर चुप रहता है। शव के पास हिसकारी और कर्कश आवाज़।

Behaviour & Ecology व्यवहार और पारिस्थितिकी

Soaring and foraging: White-rumped Vultures are obligate scavengers that spend most of their day soaring at tremendous heights (3000 m or more) in search of carcasses. They rely on thermal updrafts and can detect dead animals from enormous distances using keen eyesight. Communal feeding at carcasses is typical, with dozens or even hundreds of vultures gathering.

Diet: Exclusively carrion; primarily large livestock and wild ungulates:

  • Cattle and buffalo carcasses
  • Wild ungulates (deer, nilgai, wild boar)
  • Sometimes: birds and reptiles, especially bone
  • Cannot kill living prey; entirely dependent on dead animals

Stomach acid: Extremely powerful, capable of digesting bones within hours.

Feeding behaviour: Vultures approach carcasses cautiously, often waiting for other species to begin feeding first. Once committed, they feed voraciously and can consume up to 1.5 kg of meat in a single meal. They then may not feed again for several days.

Social behaviour: Highly gregarious, especially at carcasses. Colonial nesters on cliffs and large trees. Roosts communally on cliff faces and in large trees.

Nesting: Builds large stick nests in tall trees or on cliff faces. Breeds February–May in the region.

हिंदी: आकाश में घूमते हुए मरे पशु खोजता है। एक शव पर सैकड़ों गिद्धें बैठ सकते हैं। एक बार में बहुत खा लेता है, फिर कई दिन भूखा रह सकता है।

Life Cycle / Phenology जीवन चक्र

Resident population: In eastern UP, the White-rumped Vulture is now extremely rare and possibly only present as rare vagrants or ancient residents. Historically, it was a breeding resident.

Breeding season (historical): Bred February–June, primarily on tall neem and pipal trees and cliff faces.

Nest: Large stick nest, often reused for several years; typically 1–2 eggs laid.

Incubation and fledging: Long incubation period (approximately 50 days); fledging period 100–130 days. Young dependent for several months post-fledging.

Courtship: Display flights and nest-building activity observable from January onwards.

Moult: Prebasic moult occurs July–October.

Predators / Natural Enemies शत्रु

  • Direct predators: Adult vultures have few natural predators; chicks and eggs vulnerable to eagles, monitor lizards, and corvids.
  • Poisoning: The primary cause of population decline—diclofenac and other NSAIDs in livestock carcasses cause kidney failure and death in vultures. Lead ammunition in carcasses and pesticide residues also fatal.
  • Parasites: Intestinal helminths and blood parasites.
हिंदी: बड़ी ईगल और मॉनिटर छिपकली घोंसले पर हमला कर सकते हैं। जहरीली दवाएँ मारती हैं।

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि प्रासंगिकता

Protected status: Critically Endangered under the IUCN Red List. Protected under the Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972, Schedule I (highest protection). Killing, capturing, or poisoning is a serious criminal offense.

Ecosystem service: White-rumped Vultures were the primary scavengers in South Asian ecosystems, disposing of hundreds of tons of livestock carrion annually. Their near-extinction has created a public health crisis, as carcasses now decompose slowly and can spread disease (especially avian influenza).

Catastrophic decline: Populations declined by ~99% in 1990–2000 due to veterinary use of diclofenac (an NSAID used to treat inflammation in livestock). When cattle treated with diclofenac died, vultures consuming the carcasses accumulated lethal doses in their kidneys. A single carcass could poison dozens of vultures.

Recovery efforts: Diclofenac banned in India (2006), but meloxicam and other NSAIDs with similar effects are still used. Vulture safe zones and intensive breeding programs exist but populations remain critically low.

Current status in UP: Very rare; its present status in eastern UP is unknown, and any sighting should be documented.

हिंदी: यह विधि अधिनियम 1972 की अंतर्गत अति सुरक्षित है। यह लगभग विलुप्त हो गई है। इसे बचाना बहुत जरूरी है। दिखे तो तुरंत बताएँ।

This species is NOT a field record. The distribution below is based on published sources and historical records describing where White-rumped Vulture historically occurred in the Basti–Ayodhya region.

Historical range: White-rumped Vultures historically bred in eastern Uttar Pradesh, particularly on large trees in river valleys (especially along the Ghaghra and Rapti) and on cliffs. They were the most abundant large raptor in the region until the 1990s.

Current status: The species is now Critically Endangered and very rare, and its present status here is unknown. It has not been documented in recent surveys (Yashmita-Ulman 2022–2025; Mishra et al. 2019).

What a first record would be: Any sighting of a White-rumped Vulture in eastern UP would be scientifically significant. A dated photograph showing the dark brown body with white rump patch, in a specific location (with GPS coordinates and date), should be submitted immediately through the About page of this atlas. Nest sites with breeding evidence would be of exceptional importance.

Conservation implication: If rediscovered in the region, the location should be protected as a potential breeding or roosting site and made known to conservation organizations working on vulture recovery.

हिंदी: यह पक्षी अब यहाँ लगभग नहीं दिखता। पहले बहुत अधिक संख्या में था। अगर दिखे तो तुरंत फोटो लेकर बताएँ। यह बहुत महत्वपूर्ण है।

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Once bred from Afghanistan to central China and the entire Indian subcontinent. Now confined to a few scattered populations, primarily in central India and the Himalayas. Monotypic.

In India: Historically widespread; now reduced to fewer than 500 pairs in scattered locations, primarily in central India and the western Himalayas.

In Uttar Pradesh: Historically a common breeding resident across the state; now very rare, and its present status here is unknown.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Historically bred locally; now almost certainly absent. GBIF records are primarily historical.

Field rule: White-rumped Vulture is the largest dark brown vulture with a sharply contrasting white rump patch. The contrast between dark body and white rump is the most diagnostic feature. No other large vulture in the region shows this pattern.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. The species was described by Johann Friedrich Wilhelm Gmelin in 1788 in the Systema Naturae, based on Indian specimens. Type locality India.

Subspecies. Monotypic — no subspecies are recognized.

Family and order placement. Order Accipitriformes; family Accipitridae. The genus Gyps comprises the Old World "true" vultures, distinguished by large size, bare or sparsely feathered heads, and obligate carrion feeding.

Phylogenetic notes. Molecular work places Gyps bengalensis within a clade including Gyps himalayensis and the African White-backed Vulture (Gyps africanus). These species share a common ancestor.

IUCN status rationale. The species is Critically Endangered (CR) due to a catastrophic population decline of approximately 99% from 1990 to 2000, primarily caused by diclofenac poisoning. Although diclofenac is now banned in India, other NSAIDs with similar effects continue to be used in veterinary medicine. Populations remain at critically low levels (estimated fewer than 500 pairs globally). Recovery is dependent on continued veterinary drug regulation and intensive management of remaining populations through captive breeding and translocation programs.

हिंदी: यह गिद्ध विश्व में लगभग विलुप्त हो गई है। दवाओं से इसकी बहुत क्षति हुई है। इसे बचाने के लिए विशेष प्रयास चल रहे हैं।

Conservation Status संरक्षण स्थिति

The White-rumped Vulture is Critically Endangered and in imminent danger of extinction. Immediate actions required:

  1. Drug regulation: Ensure complete elimination of diclofenac and monitoring of other potentially toxic veterinary NSAIDs.
  2. Safe livestock zones: Establish areas where only vulture-safe veterinary drugs are used, allowing vultures to scavenge safely.
  3. Breeding programs: Continue and expand captive breeding and translocation programs.
  4. Monitoring: Regular surveys to track population trends and identify roosting and potential breeding sites.
  5. Local awareness: Community education on the importance of vultures and the dangers of veterinary drug poisoning.
