हिमालयी गिद्ध / Himalayan Griffon

Gyps himalayensis (Hume, 1873) — Accipitridae

हिमालयी गिद्ध — विशाल भूरे रंग की गिद्ध जो सर्दियों में हिमालय से मैदानों में आता है। इसके बहुत बड़े पंख, सफ़ेद गर्दन और पीली आँख इसे तुरंत पहचान देती हैं। यह आकाश में ऊंचाई पर घूमता है और मृत पशुओं को खाता है, जिससे पर्यावरण स्वच्छ रहता है।

Field tip: The largest vulture regularly seen in eastern UP. Pale brown body with whitish head and neck. Massive wingspan and buoyant soaring flight are diagnostic. Often seen in loose groups soaring high over open country. Cannot be confused with other vultures once the whitish neck and pale coloration are noted.

Morphology आकारिकी

Size: One of the world's largest vultures; 95–115 cm long; wingspan 210–260 cm. Weight 4.5–6.5 kg. Only slightly smaller than the Indian Vulture historically, but now more common than the nearly extinct Gyps indicus.

Plumage: Overall pale brown or greyish-brown, with darker flight feathers and back coverts. Rump and upper tail coverts pale. Underparts cream or pale buff. Immatures darker brown overall.

Head and neck: Largely whitish or buff, sparsely feathered (typical vulture adaptation for feeding on carcasses). Skin of head yellowish. Face bare of feathers except for fine down.

Eyes: Yellow or pale yellow; large and forward-facing, adapted for spotting carcasses from great heights.

Bill: Long, strong, hooked at tip, yellowish with dark tip. Adapted for tearing tough hide and flesh.

Legs and feet: Powerful, yellowish; talons curved and strong for gripping carcasses.

Wings: Very long and broad, reaching well past tail at rest. Primary feathers show striking contrast in flight.

Sexes: Females slightly larger than males; plumage very similar.

Juveniles: Darker brown overall; head darker; underparts more heavily streaked.

Vocalisations. Call repertoire (largely silent at distance):

  1. Hissing and croaking: At carcass or nest; low-frequency sounds not usually heard in flight.
  2. Wing noise: Loud flapping when taking off or in competitive feeding.
  3. Silent soaring: Largely silent in flight.
हिंदी: ज़्यादातर चुप रहता है। शव के पास "स" की आवाज़ करता है। पंखों की तेज़ आवाज़।

Behaviour & Ecology व्यवहार और पारिस्थितिकी

Soaring and foraging: Himalayan Griffons spend most of daylight hours soaring at great heights (up to 3000 m or more), watching the ground for dead animals. They rely on thermal updrafts and can detect carcasses from enormous distances. A single carcass may attract dozens of vultures from a radius of many kilometers.

Diet: Exclusively carrion (dead animals). Feed on:

  • Livestock carcasses
  • Wild ungulate carcasses (deer, wild boar)
  • Bird carcasses
  • Occasionally: placental tissues and bones

Feeding behaviour: Vultures approach a carcass cautiously, landing at distance first. Once feeding begins, hundreds may gather within hours. They consume large meals (up to 1.5 kg of meat in a single feeding) and can then survive for many days without food. Stomach acid is extremely powerful and can dissolve bones.

Social behaviour: Gregarious, especially at carcasses. On the wing, flocks may include dozens of birds. Nocturnal roosts are on cliff faces or large trees.

Nesting: Winter visitors do not breed in the region; breeding occurs in Himalayan cliffs from March onwards.

Predators: Adult vultures have few predators; chicks and eggs vulnerable to corvids and eagles on breeding grounds.

हिंदी: आकाश में घूमते हुए शव खोजता है। झुंड में बैठकर खाता है। एक बार में बहुत भोजन करता है, फिर कई दिन खा नहीं सकता।

Life Cycle / Phenology जीवन चक्र

Winter visitor: Absent during breeding season (March–September); returns to eastern UP from October and remains through February or March.

Arrival and departure: First birds appear in late September or early October; numbers peak in November–December; departure begins in February, with most birds gone by late March.

Breeding grounds: Breeding range in the Himalayas from Kashmir to Bhutan; no breeding in UP.

Roosts: Nocturnal roosts on cliff faces, high-rise buildings, or large trees overlooking open plains.

Moult: Prebasic moult occurs before migration; birds arrive in non-breeding plumage.

Predators / Natural Enemies शत्रु

  • Direct predators: Adult Himalayan Griffons have few predators; eagles may harass them in flight.
  • At nest: Lammergeier and crows predating eggs and chicks (on Himalayan breeding cliffs).
  • Poisoning: Accidental poisoning from lead ammunition in carcasses and pesticides in livestock.
  • Parasites: Intestinal helminths and blood parasites.
हिंदी: बड़ी ईगल इसे डरा सकती है। घोंसले में कौवे अंडे खा जाते हैं। जहरीली दवा से मार जाता है।

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि प्रासंगिकता

Protected status: Protected under the Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972. The Himalayan Griffon, along with other Gyps vultures, is classified as critically important for ecosystem health. Vultures are the primary scavengers in many ecosystems and prevent the spread of disease.

Sanitation role: In the past, vultures disposed of hundreds of tons of livestock carrion annually, providing a free and efficient sanitation service. This ecological service is irreplaceable and has been severely disrupted by vulture population declines.

Historical persecution: Vultures were historically killed as pests or by hunters, but this is now illegal.

Livestock impact: Vultures occasionally harass weak or dying livestock but do not directly predate healthy animals. This behaviour is rare and misunderstood.

Conservation value: The species is Near Threatened (NT) primarily due to historic persecution and habitat loss. In India, the Indian Vulture (Gyps indicus) and White-backed Vulture (Gyps bengalensis) have suffered catastrophic declines from veterinary drug poisoning, and the Himalayan Griffon now fills part of that ecological role.

This species is NOT a field record. The distribution below is based on published sources describing where Himalayan Griffon should be looked for in the Basti–Ayodhya region and where it has been documented by ornithologists in published surveys.

Winter range: Expected in eastern Uttar Pradesh from October to March, particularly over open plains and the Ghaghra and Rapti river valleys where livestock husbandry provides regular carcass availability.

Habitat preference: Open, rolling terrain with scattered villages and pastoral areas. River valleys where updrafts facilitate soaring and where carcasses are found.

Peak occurrence: November–December, when populations from the Himalayas have completed southward migration.

Published records: GBIF holds published records from these districts.

What a first record would be: A dated photograph or detailed observation showing the bird clearly in a specific location (with GPS coordinates and date) submitted through the About page. Binoculars from ground and video from aircraft are the typical detection methods; direct observation is straightforward given the species' massive size.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Breeds in the Himalayas from Kashmir to Bhutan. Winters across the Indian subcontinent, Central Asia, and Afghanistan. Monotypic (no subspecies recognized).

In India: Breeds in the Himalayas; winters throughout the Indian plains, particularly in the Gangetic region.

In Uttar Pradesh: Regular winter visitor across the state, particularly in the Gangetic floodplains and river valleys.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Regular winter visitor over open plains and river valleys. GBIF holds published records from the wider Awadh–Purvanchal plain.

Field rule: Himalayan Griffon is the largest and palest vulture regularly seen, with pale brown body, whitish neck, and yellow eye. If it is darker brown overall, it is likely White-rumped Vulture. If it has a distinctive white rump patch, it is definitely White-rumped, not Himalayan.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. The species was described by Allan Octavian Hume in 1873. The original publication and type locality details are found in Stray Feathers (Hume's journal of ornithological observations from India).

Subspecies. Monotypic — no subspecies are currently recognized.

Family and order placement. Order Accipitriformes; family Accipitridae (eagles, hawks, vultures). The genus Gyps comprises the Old World "true" vultures, characterized by their large size, bare or sparsely feathered heads, and carrion-feeding specialization.

Phylogenetic notes. Molecular work places Gyps himalayensis within a clade including other Old World vultures such as the White-backed and White-rumped Vultures. It is closely related to G. bengalensis and G. indicus.

IUCN status rationale. The species is assessed as Near Threatened (NT) globally, primarily due to historical persecution, habitat loss, and accidental poisoning (particularly from lead ammunition in carcasses). Population trends are declining in some regions but stable in others. The species remains widespread across its range and is not imminently threatened with extinction, but long-term population viability depends on conservation of carcass availability and reduction of poisoning threats.
