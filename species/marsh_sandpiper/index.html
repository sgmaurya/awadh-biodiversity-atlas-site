मार्श सैंडपाइपर / Marsh Sandpiper

Tringa stagnatilis (Brehm, 1831) — Scolopacidae

मार्श सैंडपाइपर — सर्दियों में मध्य एशिया और साइबेरिया से आने वाली सबसे छोटी शाखी, जो उथले तालाबों, नहरों और धान के खेतों में कीड़े खोजते हुए अकेले या छोटे झुंड में दिखता है। इसकी लंबी टाँगें, पतली चोंच और भूरे-सफ़ेद रंग की पहचान हैं। यह बहुत तेजी से और चुस्ती से पानी में शिकार करता है।

Field tip: The smallest and most delicate sandpiper in eastern UP, with disproportionately long legs and a needle-thin bill. Walks carefully in shallow water, probing for small aquatic insects. The greenish-yellow legs and white rump in flight are diagnostic. Usually feeds alone or in pairs, unlike larger sandpipers that flock.

Morphology आकारिकी

Size: 22–25 cm long; wingspan 37–43 cm. Weight 30–45 g. Smallest sandpiper regularly observed in eastern UP.

Plumage: Pale grey-brown above with fine darker streaking. Whitish supercilium (eyebrow stripe). Underparts and neck white. Rump white and conspicuous in flight. Tail dark with white sides. No seasonal plumage change; winter and breeding plumage similar.

Bill: Extremely long (35–42 mm) and thin, slightly drooped toward tip, dark above and pale below. Specialized for probing into soft mud and shallow water.

Legs and feet: Disproportionately long (32–38 mm tarsus), greenish-yellow; key field mark separating it from similar sandpipers.

Wings: When folded, wings just reach tip of tail; pointed wings for swift flight.

Eyes: Dark and alert; face somewhat lacking expression.

Sexes: Females slightly larger than males; plumage very similar.

Vocalisations. Call repertoire:

  1. Contact call: A soft, high-pitched chu-wit or chit, given in flight and when flushed.
  2. Alarm call: A sharper, repeated chip-chip-chip.
  3. Song: Not well documented; likely given on breeding grounds in Siberia.
हिंदी: नर्म सीटी जैसी आवाज़ "चु-विट"। डर जाने पर तेज़ "चिप-चिप"।

Behaviour & Ecology व्यवहार और पारिस्थितिकी

Foraging: Marsh Sandpipers are solitary or in small groups of 2–6 birds. They walk deliberately in shallow water, probing the mud and sediment with their long bills. Diet consists entirely of small aquatic invertebrates:

  • Dipteran larvae and pupae (midges, flies)
  • Small crustaceans
  • Aquatic insect nymphs
  • Tiny gastropods

Habitat preference: Shallow margins of lakes, tanks, and marshes with soft mud. Particularly attracted to areas with sparse emergent vegetation. Rice paddies during winter flooding provide excellent feeding habitat.

Feeding time: Primarily diurnal feeders; most active in early morning and late afternoon.

Social behaviour: Unlike larger sandpipers, they are not gregarious. Usually seen alone or in pairs; small groups may form at productive feeding sites but remain dispersed.

Flight: Light and buoyant, with rapid wing beats. Often calls while flying. White rump and sides of tail are conspicuous in flight.

Predators: Raptors such as Peregrine Falcon and small eagles, and possibly corvids.

हिंदी: अकेले या छोटे झुंड में। बहुत धीरे-धीरे पानी में शिकार करता है। छोटे कीड़े खाता है। डर जाने पर तेजी से उड़ जाता है।

Life Cycle / Phenology जीवन चक्र

Winter visitor: Absent during breeding season (May–August); returns to eastern UP from October and remains through April.

Arrival and departure: First birds arrive in late September or early October; numbers peak in November–February; departure begins in March, with most birds gone by late April.

Breeding grounds: Breeding range in central Siberia and central Asian steppes; no breeding in India.

Roosts: Nocturnal roosts on open water bodies or mudbanks. Day feeding sites often different from roosts; birds commute daily.

Moult: Prebasic moult occurs before migration; birds arrive in non-breeding plumage and maintain it throughout winter stay.

हिंदी: अक्टूबर से अप्रैल तक रहता है। सर्दी की नहरों और तालाबों में मिलता है। साइबेरिया में प्रजनन करता है।

Predators / Natural Enemies शत्रु

  • Avian predators: Peregrine Falcon, Booted Eagle, Crows
  • Mammalian predators: Rarely taken, but foxes and wild boar may predate if encountered
  • Parasites: Intestinal helminths and protozoa typical of migratory shorebirds
हिंदी: शाहीन बाज़ और ईगल इसका शिकार करते हैं।

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि प्रासंगिकता

Protected status: The Marsh Sandpiper is a game bird in some countries but is protected under the Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972 in India. Hunting is prohibited.

Agricultural context: Winters in rice paddies and wetlands that are under human use. The species benefits from the flooding pattern of winter irrigation and rice cultivation. Damage to crops is negligible; the bird feeds on aquatic invertebrates, not plant material.

Scientific value: Migratory sandpipers are important indicators of wetland health and quality. Population monitoring on wintering grounds provides data on breeding ground productivity in central Asia.

This species is NOT a field record. The distribution below is based on published sources describing where Marsh Sandpiper should be looked for in the Basti–Ayodhya region and where it has been documented by ornithologists in published surveys.

Winter range: Expected in eastern Uttar Pradesh from October to April, particularly on the Ghaghra and Rapti river systems and their associated wetlands. Shallow tanks and marshes in Basti, Ayodhya, and adjacent districts provide suitable habitat.

Habitat preference: Shallow water bodies with soft muddy margins and minimal disturbance. Flood plains and irrigation channels during winter flooding.

Peak occurrence: November–February, when populations from central Asia have completed southward migration.

Published records: GBIF holds published records from these districts.

What a first record would be: A dated photograph showing the bird clearly in a specific location (with GPS coordinates and date) submitted through the About page of this atlas. The long legs and fine bill are distinctive; identification to species is reliable with good images.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Breeds in central Siberia, central Asia, and parts of Russia and Kazakhstan. Winters in Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, and Indian subcontinent. Highly migratory with no subspecies recognized.

In India: Distributed throughout the Indian subcontinent as a winter visitor (October–April) on all wetland systems.

In Uttar Pradesh: Occurs widely in winter, particularly in the Gangetic floodplains and along major river systems.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Regular winter visitor on the Ghaghra and Rapti river systems and associated wetlands; expected in suitable habitat.

Field rule: Marsh Sandpiper is the smallest and most delicate sandpiper, with disproportionately long greenish-yellow legs, an extremely fine bill, and pale plain plumage. If the bird is larger or has red legs, it is a different species. If it bobs its tail, it is a Common Sandpiper.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. The species was first described by Christian Ludwig Brehm in 1831 as Tringa stagnatilis. The original publication details and type locality are not definitively established in modern taxonomic literature; see GBIF for complete nomenclatural history.

Subspecies. Monotypic — no subspecies are currently recognized.

Family and order placement. Order Charadriiformes; family Scolopacidae (sandpipers, phalaropes, and snipes). The genus Tringa contains the "shanks" and typical sandpipers.

Phylogenetic notes. Marsh Sandpiper is closely related to other Tringa species, particularly the Spotted Redshank and Greenshank. Molecular work places it within a clade of migratory Eurasian shorebirds.

IUCN status rationale. The species is assessed as Least Concern (LC) globally. Populations appear stable, and the species is widely distributed across a vast breeding and wintering range. It is adaptable to a variety of wetland types and is not threatened by hunting in most of its range (protected in India).
