गोह / Bengal Monitor

Varanus bengalensis (Daudin, 1802) — Varanidae

गोह भारत की सबसे बड़ी छिपकली है — 1.75 मीटर तक लम्बी, भारी-भरकम, ताकतवर। कोमोडो ड्रैगन का करीबी रिश्तेदार। पूर्वी UP के खेतों, नदी किनारों और गाँव के आसपास यह मिलती है। इसे देखकर लोग डरते हैं, पर यह इंसान पर हमला नहीं करती। गोह अनुसूची I की संरक्षित प्रजाति है — बाघ के बराबर कानूनी संरक्षण — फिर भी इसके वसा और चमड़े के लिए सबसे अधिक शिकार होने वाले जानवरों में से एक है।

Quick Identification त्वरित पहचान

FeatureDescription
SizeUp to 175 cm total length; typical adults 70–120 cm
ColourOlive-grey to dark brown with yellowish spots arranged in ocelli; paler below
TongueForked, deep blue/black — constantly flickering (chemoreception)
HeadElongated, narrow, pointed snout; neck as long as head
TailCylindrical (not laterally compressed as in V. salvator); ~1.5× body length
WalkQuadrupedal; deliberate; can run quickly over short distances

Field tip: A large lizard with a forked tongue is unmistakably a monitor — no other lizard in UP is this large. The forked tongue flickering constantly distinguishes it even at distance. Garden lizard, chameleon, and skinks are all much smaller.

Morphology आकारिकी

FeatureMeasurement
Maximum total length175 cm

Body: Powerful, muscular, low-slung. Scales are small and beaded (not overlapping like snake scales). Overall body surface appears coarsely granular. Heavily built limbs with strong claws — adapted for digging and climbing.

Skin pattern: Olive to dark brown or blackish dorsum. Yellowish spots or ocelli on back and sides — highly variable between individuals and with age (fades in old animals). Underside cream to yellowish. Throat sometimes spotted.

Tongue: Deep blue-black, forked, very long. Flickers constantly — each extension samples chemical molecules from the air/ground. The forked tips enter the Jacobson's organ in the palate, giving directional chemical information (like two nostrils providing stereo smell).

Head: Snout is long and somewhat depressed. Eyes with rounded pupils — good colour vision. Ear opening visible behind the eye.

Tail: Long, cylindrical, muscular. Used as a weapon (powerful whip) when threatened. Distinguished from the water monitor (V. salvator) by this round (not laterally keeled) tail.

Ecological Role पारिस्थितिक भूमिका

Opportunistic apex predator: Bengal Monitor has one of the broadest diets of any reptile in UP:

  • Eggs (bird, reptile, turtle) — a major food source; regularly digs up bird nests
  • Frogs and toads (particularly Hoplobatrachus tigerinus, AMP-0002)
  • Snakes (including venomous species)
  • Small mammals (rodents, hares)
  • Lizards (including smaller monitors)
  • Large insects and beetles
  • Carrion — an important scavenging role alongside kites and vultures
  • Young birds

Burrow ecology: Bengal Monitors excavate large burrows (1–3 m long) for thermoregulation and egg-laying. These burrows, once vacated, are used by many other species: foxes, hares, small cats, snakes, and ground-nesting birds. Monitor burrows are a significant microhabitat resource.

Pest control: By consuming large numbers of rodents, frogs, and large insects, monitors provide measurable pest control services in agricultural areas.

Termite mound nesting: Bengal Monitors preferentially lay eggs inside termite mounds (Odontotermes sp., SFL-0001) — the mound provides stable temperature and humidity for incubation, and termites may repair the breach, creating a perfect incubation chamber.

Growth: Young monitors grow rapidly — doubling in size in the first year. Sexual maturity at 2–3 years. Lifespan: 15–20 years in wild.

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि एवं मानव उपयोग

Legal status: Schedule I, Wildlife Protection Act 1972 — highest protection category, equal to tiger and elephant. Despite this, poaching continues because:

  1. Monitor fat (oil) is highly sought in traditional medicine — claimed to cure joint pain, rheumatism, skin diseases. No clinical validation.
  2. Monitor skin is used in traditional drums (kanjira, dholak leather) and leather goods — a significant illegal trade.
  3. Some communities use the meat as food.

Poaching and trade: A TRAFFIC report (2014) estimated tens of thousands of Bengal Monitors are killed annually in India for fat and skin. UP is one of the high-pressure states.

Rodent control service: In agricultural areas, a single large monitor can consume dozens of rodents per month — providing economic pest control. This is lost when monitors are killed.

Conflict: Poultry raids cause conflict — a monitor in a poultry shed can take several birds quickly. However, a monitor regularly kills rodents that would otherwise eat far more grain than the monitor takes in birds.

Seen on Saryu riverbanks, agricultural field margins, and near village ponds in Basti district. Known to raid poultry locally — farmers describe "goha" attacks in chicken coops. Termite mound excavations in post-monsoon surveys occasionally reveal monitor eggs. Road mortality is significant — large, slow to escape vehicles, frequently killed on main roads. A large specimen (estimated 120+ cm) was seen on a field bund near Basti town in March, suggesting a breeding adult in good condition.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Indian subcontinent and eastward through Myanmar to Southeast Asia (Thailand, Malaysia, Vietnam). Found from Afghanistan and Pakistan in the west through India, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, and into Southeast Asia.

In India: One of the most widely distributed lizards in India — found from the Himalayan foothills to the southern tip, and from the western deserts to the eastern coast. Absent only from the high-altitude Himalayan zone and true dense rainforest.

In Uttar Pradesh: Distributed across the entire state in agricultural, scrub, and riverine habitats. Common along all major river systems and in the Terai forest zone.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Year-round resident; most active March–November. Found along Saryu, Rapti, and Kuwano riverbanks, in agricultural scrub, and at village margins.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. Varanus bengalensis was originally described by François Marie Daudin in 1802 in Histoire naturelle générale et particulière des reptiles, Vol. 3, as Tupinambis bengalensis. The species was subsequently transferred to the genus Varanus Merrem, 1820. The authority is therefore cited with parentheses: (Daudin, 1802). The species name bengalensis refers to Bengal, the geographic region from which the original type specimen was collected.

Subspecies. Three subspecies have been recognised:

SubspeciesRange
V. b. bengalensisIndian subcontinent (nominate — includes UP)
V. b. nebulosusThailand, Myanmar, Malaysia
V. b. irrawadicusIrrawaddy basin, Myanmar

Family and order placement. Belongs to the order Squamata, family Varanidae (monitor lizards). The family Varanidae contains a single living genus (Varanus) with approximately 80 species. Varanids are the sister group to mosasaurs (extinct marine lizards) and represent the closest living relatives of snakes within the Squamata. The genus Varanus is remarkable for its metabolic rate, active foraging behaviour, and intelligence — notably, monitor lizards are the only non-avian reptiles known to understand numbers.

Phylogenetic notes. Molecular phylogenetics places Varanus bengalensis within the Varanus bengalensis species complex (Bengal monitors of South and Southeast Asia). The family Varanidae is distributed across Africa, Asia, and Australia (with the Komodo Dragon, V. komodoensis, as its largest living member). Varanid lizards are unusual among reptiles in having a high aerobic scope, allowing sustained activity — a metabolic feature more similar to mammals and birds than to other reptiles.

IUCN status rationale. Assessed as Least Concern (LC) globally by the IUCN Red List. Widespread and common in undisturbed areas. However, intensive poaching for fat, skin, and meat poses serious threats to local populations in India. Elevated to Schedule I (highest protection) in India under WPA 1972.
