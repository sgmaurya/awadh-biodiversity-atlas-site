हाजी लगलग / White Stork

Ciconia ciconia (Linnaeus, 1758) — Ciconiidae

हाजी लगलग — एक बड़ा, सफेद और काले पंखों वाला सारस जो अक्टूबर-नवंबर में मध्य एशिया से आता है और मार्च तक रहता है। इसका लाल ठोंग और लंबी काली और सफेद गर्दन इसे तुरंत पहचान देते हैं। यह नदियों और तालाबों के पास खड़े होकर मछलियाँ और मेंढक पकड़ता है।

Field tip: A very large white stork with black wing feathers and bright red legs and bill — is a White Stork. Unmistakable in size and appearance. Walks slowly in shallow water and wet grasslands, hunting for fish, frogs, and other prey. Soars on thermals with neck and legs extended.

Size: 100–115 cm long; wingspan 200–220 cm. Weight 2300–4500 g (males heavier).

Plumage: Predominantly white. Flight feathers (primaries) and wings black. Greater wing coverts black. Tail white. Undertail coverts white. Some individuals show slight buff wash on back and rump. Sexes similar in plumage.

Head and neck: White and slender. Head proportionally small. Neck long and flexible for hunting.

Bill: Long (110–130 mm), pointed, red or orange-red with slight black tip.

Legs and feet: Exceptionally long (130–160 mm), red or orange-red; unfeathered tarsus and tibia. Webbing between toes minimal (storks are not swimmers).

Eyes: Dark brown; small, placed on sides of head.

Vocalisations: Mostly silent; clattering sounds from bill:

  1. Bill clattering: Loud, rapid clattering sound produced by rapidly opening and closing the bill; given as greeting call and during courtship. Audible at distance.
  2. Hissing: Soft hissing when defending nest or young.
हिंदी: चोंच को तेज़ी से खड़-खड़ करता है — "क्लैटर" की आवाज़।

Behaviour & Ecology व्यवहार और पारिस्थितिकी

Carnivorous hunter: White Storks are skilled hunters that feed on:

  • Fish (primary prey; all sizes)
  • Frogs and toads (important diet component)
  • Small mammals (mice, voles, shrews)
  • Aquatic insects and larvae
  • Small snakes and lizards
  • Crustaceans (crabs, crayfish)

Hunting behaviour: Walk slowly and deliberately in shallow water, mud, and wet grasslands. Strike rapidly with long bills when prey detected. Hunt during daytime, particularly at dawn and dusk.

Social behaviour: Solitary or in pairs during winter in Asia (unlike European populations that roost communally). Highly territorial while hunting; individuals defend feeding territories.

Roosting: Nocturnal roosts in tall trees near water bodies; return to same roosting sites nightly.

Flight: Excellent soarers; use thermal currents to gain altitude. Fly with neck and legs extended (unlike herons which tuck neck).

Predators: Eagle species may take young; humans historically hunted for food.

हिंदी: अकेले या जोड़ों में शिकार करते हैं। मछलियाँ और मेंढक मुख्य शिकार। धीमे चाल से चलता है।

Life Cycle / Phenology जीवन चक्र

Winter visitor: Arrives October–November from breeding grounds; departs by March–April. Does not breed in India.

Breeding range: Central and eastern Europe, western Asia (Caucasus, Kazakhstan, southern Russia). Breeds March–August at elevation 0–2000 m.

Migration: Breeds in Europe and Central Asia; southward migration begins August. Travels through Middle East to winter in Africa and South Asia. Arrives in Indian subcontinent October–November; most depart by April.

Wintering: Distributed in river valleys and wetland systems; solitary or in small groups. Widely ranging within winter habitat; not tightly territorial.

Moult: Gradual moult during wintering season; acquire fresh plumage for next breeding season.

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि प्रासंगिकता

Pest control: White Storks provide an ecological service by consuming large numbers of frogs and small vertebrates, though impact on crop pests is minimal.

Cultural and religious significance: In European folklore, the White Stork is a symbol of fertility and childbirth ("the stork brings babies"). In Islamic cultures, the name "لقلق" (laqlaaq) or "हाजी लगलग" suggests pilgrimages, as the bird was believed to make an annual pilgrimage. In Hindu tradition, the bird is associated with devotion and duty.

Habitat requirement: Dependent on wetland systems with adequate shallow water and prey abundance.

White Storks are expected in the Basti–Ayodhya region along river systems and larger wetlands from October through March. Mishra et al. (2019) recorded this species at Bakhira Bird Sanctuary during October 2017–March 2018. Yashmita-Ulman & Singh (2025) recorded it at Parwati Arga from March 2024–February 2025. Published range and habitat information place this species in river valleys and wetland systems during winter months, particularly along the Ghaghra and Rapti river systems.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Breeds in Europe and western Asia; winters across Africa and South Asia. Two populations: Western European (wintering in Africa) and Eastern European/Asian (wintering in South Asia and Africa).

In India: Regular winter visitor to northern regions, particularly in the Indo-Gangetic plain.

In Uttar Pradesh: Winter visitor to river systems and major wetlands, with records from across the state.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Winter visitor to the Ghaghra and Rapti river systems and adjoining wetlands. Recorded at Bakhira Bird Sanctuary and Parwati Arga. Present October–March, with peak presence November–February.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. Ciconia ciconia was described by Carl Linnaeus in 1758 in Systema Naturae, 10th Edition, volume 1, page 142, as Ardea ciconia. Type locality: Europe.

Subspecies. Two subspecies are recognised:

  • C. c. ciconia Linnaeus, 1758 — Europe and western Asia (occurs in India)
  • C. c. asiatica Severtzov, 1873 — Asia (Kazakhstan to China); rare in India

Populations wintering in India belong primarily to C. c. ciconia.

Family and order placement. Order Ciconiiformes; family Ciconiidae (storks). The family contains 19 species globally.

Phylogenetic notes. Ciconia ciconia is closely related to other Ciconia species, including the Asian Woolly-necked Stork (C. episcopus) and Black Stork (C. nigra). Molecular evidence supports monophyly of the genus.

IUCN status rationale. Assessed as Least Concern (LC). European populations have declined historically due to habitat loss and persecution, but populations are now stable in areas with wetland protection. Asian populations remain relatively stable.
