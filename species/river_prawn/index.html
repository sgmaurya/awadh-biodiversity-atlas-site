गंगा झींगा / Ganga River Prawn

Macrobrachium gangeticum Spence Bate, 1868 — Palaemonidae

गंगा झींगा — घाघरा और राप्ती का मूल निवासी बड़ा मीठे-पानी का झींगा। पारदर्शी भूरे-जैतूनी रंग का बदन, नीले-धूसर बड़े पंजे (नर में), लंबा ऊपर की ओर मुड़ा हुआ सिर-काँटा। रात को चारा चुनता है; दिन में लकड़ी-पत्थर के नीचे छुपता है। मल्लाह और निषाद समुदाय के जाल से पकड़ा जाता है — बाज़ार में मछली से महँगा बिकता है। यह खारे पानी से पैदा नहीं होता — पूरा जीवनचक्र मीठे पानी में पूरा करता है, इसीलिए यह घाघरा का असली नदी झींगा है। जहाँ झींगा है, वहाँ पानी अभी ठीक है।

Field tip: In Ghaghara/Rapti fish markets April–June and October–November, the large grey-olive prawns with elongated blue-grey claws and a prominent upward-curved rostrum are Macrobrachium gangeticum — the genuine wild river prawn. Larger and differently shaped from farmed M. rosenbergii. At night, a torch shone along a shallow river margin may reveal their eyes as tiny reflective points.

Morphology आकारिकी

Biometrics (typical adult populations in the Ganga river basin):

FeatureMeasurement
Carapace length (CL)4.0–6.8 cm
Total body length (excluding claws)10–20 cm
Second cheliped length (adult male)12–25 cm
Weight (typical adult)40–120 g
  • Body: Laterally compressed; translucent grey-olive with faint blue-green tints; scattered darker mottling for camouflage
  • Rostrum: Long, upward-curved with teeth on both dorsal and ventral edges — longer rostrum than most congeners
  • Chelipeds (large claws): Second pair greatly enlarged and elongated in adult males; used for combat, prey capture, and mate guarding; banded blue-grey to brownish
  • Walking legs: 3 pairs of smaller pereopods for substrate contact; adapted for rocky and sandy river bottom
  • Pleopods: Fan-like swimming appendages; females carry eggs attached to these
  • Tail fan (uropods + telson): Broad; used for powerful backward escape thrust
  • Size: Female 10–15 cm; male 15–20 cm; males with elongated chelipeds appear much larger
  • Colour variation: Animals from clear rivers are more translucent; those from turbid channels are darker and more mottled

Ecological Role पारिस्थितिक भूमिका

Apex invertebrate predator of benthic communities. Macrobrachium gangeticum occupies a high trophic position in the riverbed food web — consuming insect larvae (mayfly, stonefly, caddisfly nymphs), small mollusks, worms, fish eggs, carrion, and plant detritus. Large adults can take small fish and tadpoles. As a generalist omnivore, it regulates the abundance of benthic invertebrate communities in the river channels where it is found.

Scavenger and carrion processor. Prawns rapidly locate and consume dead fish and other organic debris — performing an essential sanitation role in river systems. In rivers with declining fish populations, scavenger prawns help prevent organic overload and maintain water oxygen levels.

Prey for commercially important fish. Juvenile prawns are a critical food source for Labeo rohita (Rohu, FSH-0001), Catla catla (FSH-0002), and other major food fishes of the Ganga system. The prawn population directly supports the productivity of the fisheries that UP communities depend on.

Bioturbation. Foraging activity stirs and aerates river sediments, preventing sulphide accumulation in anoxic bottom muds and releasing nutrients into the water column.

Endemic to Ganga — indicator of river health. M. gangeticum is endemic to the Ganga river system. Its presence in a river reach indicates acceptable water quality, adequate dissolved oxygen, and an intact benthic invertebrate community. Disappearance signals serious habitat degradation.

Life Cycle / Phenology जीवन चक्र

  • Completely freshwater lifecycle: Unlike coastal Macrobrachium species (including the farmed M. rosenbergii), M. gangeticum does NOT require brackish water at any stage — larvae hatch, develop, and settle entirely in freshwater
  • Breeding season: Year-round with peaks pre-monsoon (April–May) and post-monsoon (October); females may breed 2–3 times per year
  • Eggs: Female carries 500–2,000 eggs attached to pleopods; eggs olive-green when fresh, darkening as they develop
  • Larval development: Passes through 8–11 zoeal stages in open water; all stages tolerate fresh water (key biological distinction from M. rosenbergii)
  • Settlement: Post-larvae settle to river substrate; rapid growth in first year
  • Sexual maturity: Males at 6–8 months; females at 8–10 months
  • Lifespan: 3–5 years; older males develop extremely elongated chelipeds
  • Activity: Nocturnal; retreats to cover (submerged logs, rock crevices, river bank undercuts) during the day

Host Plants or Prey आहार

Prey / food items (omnivore):

  • Aquatic insect larvae (Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera nymphs)
  • Small mollusks — Lamellidens marginalis juveniles (MOL-0002)
  • Freshwater worms and oligochaetes
  • Fish eggs and small fish
  • Carrion and dead organic matter
  • Aquatic plant material and algae (plant matter less preferred)
  • Freshwater crab (Barytelphusa guerini, CRU-0002) juveniles — competition and occasional predation

Predators / Natural Enemies शत्रु

  • Common Kingfisher (Alcedo atthis, BRD-0010) — actively hunts juvenile prawns in shallow river margins; a primary bird predator
  • Indian Pond Heron (Ardeola grayii, BRD-0004) — wades for prawns in shallows
  • Indian Rat Snake (Ptyas mucosa, REP-0002) — hunts in river margins
  • Large fish — adult Rohu, Catla, and predatory catfishes consume prawns
  • Otters (where present) — major predators of large adult prawns
  • Humans — primary commercial threat; highly prized in local markets

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि प्रासंगिकता

Fisheries — wild harvest. Macrobrachium gangeticum is an important component of the wild riverine fishery along the Ghaghara and Rapti rivers in Basti district. Fishing communities (Mallah, Nishad) harvest prawns using cast nets, traps, and barrier fishing during night hours. The species commands a higher price than most fish in local markets — prawn (jhinga) is considered a premium food item.

Distinguishing from farmed species. The farmed prawn commonly sold in UP markets is Macrobrachium rosenbergii (raised in aquaculture ponds using hatchery-produced larvae that require brackish water). Wild-caught M. gangeticum from Ghaghara is the native river prawn. Local fishermen distinguish between them by size, claw shape, and origin.

Nutrition and food security. Wild prawns provide high-quality protein, omega-3 fatty acids, and minerals (zinc, selenium) to fishing communities. For riparian households, prawn harvesting provides both food and supplemental income. Declining prawn catches are therefore a direct livelihood and nutrition security issue.

Conservation pressure. Major threats to M. gangeticum in the Ghaghara basin:

  • Sand dredging — destroys benthic habitat and nursery grounds
  • Pesticide runoff — insecticides (chlorpyrifos, profenofos) from rice fields are acutely toxic to crustaceans at field-edge concentrations
  • Overfishing — no size limits or seasonal closure currently enforced in UP inland fisheries
  • Upstream dams — alter flood pulse and sediment regime that structures benthic communities

Aquaculture potential. Unlike M. rosenbergii, which requires expensive brackish-water hatchery infrastructure, M. gangeticum can theoretically be cultured in entirely freshwater systems — making it a more appropriate species for small-scale freshwater prawn farming in inland UP.

Wild prawns are regularly caught in the Ghaghara and Rapti rivers near Basti town, particularly during night fishing expeditions using cast nets from boats or from the bank at low-water margins. Prawns are most commonly encountered in the catch from April to June (pre-monsoon) and again in October–November. They are sold fresh in local markets and also dried for storage. The largest individuals with elongated blue claws are male adults — visually dramatic and immediately recognisable. Village children sometimes catch small prawns in shallow stream margins using hand nets made of sari cloth.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Endemic to the northern and eastern regions of the Indian subcontinent, specifically the Ganges-Brahmaputra-Meghna river basins in India, Nepal, and Bangladesh.

In India: Restricted to the middle and upper stretches of the Ganges river system and its tributaries, primarily in the states of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and West Bengal.

In Uttar Pradesh: Distributed in the major perennial rivers of the Gangetic plain, including the Ganges, Yamuna, Ghaghara, Rapti, and Kuwano rivers.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Commonly recorded in the Ghaghara, Rapti, and Kuwano rivers, particularly along slow-flowing stretches with sandy or rocky substrates.

Range notes: No significant range changes documented.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. Macrobrachium gangeticum was described by Spence Bate in 1868 in Proceedings of the Zoological Society of London, 1868, p. 363. Type locality: Patna, Ganga River, India. The genus name Macrobrachium is derived from Ancient Greek makros (long) and brachion (arm), referring to the elongated claws.

Subspecies. Monotypic — no subspecies are currently recognised.

Family and order placement. Placed in the order Decapoda, infraorder Caridea, family Palaemonidae. This family includes a vast number of freshwater and marine shrimp and prawns. The genus Macrobrachium contains over 240 species, representing one of the most diverse groups of freshwater decapod crustaceans in the world.

Phylogenetic notes. Macrobrachium gangeticum is phylogenetically closely related to other large-bodied freshwater prawns of the Indian subcontinent, such as M. malcolmsonii and M. rosenbergii. No major taxonomic controversies are currently active for this species.

IUCN status rationale. This species has not been formally assessed by the IUCN Red List (Not Evaluated). Based on local fisheries data, populations in the Gangetic plain appear to be declining due to habitat degradation, sand mining, water pollution, and overexploitation.
