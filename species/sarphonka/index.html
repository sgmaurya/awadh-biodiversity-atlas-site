सरफोंका / Wild Indigo

Tephrosia purpurea (L.) Pers. — Fabaceae

सरफोंका — उत्तरी भारत का सबसे आम सड़क-किनारे की बूटी है, पर कुछ लोग ही इसे पहचानते हैं। बसंत में इसके छोटे-छोटे गुलाबी-बैंगनी फूल खिलते हैं, और गर्मियों में पक्षी और कीड़े इसके पत्तों पर निर्भर करते हैं। पारंपरिक रूप से इसे कीटनाशक के रूप में जाना जाता है — बीजों को पीस कर तालाबों में डाला जाता था मछली पकड़ने के लिए — और गाँवों में खुजली और त्वचा रोगों के इलाज में भी प्रयोग होता है।

Field tip: A soft-hairy, feathery-leaved herb of roadsides and waste ground, with small pink-purple pea-like flowers and slender, hairy seed pods. The compound leaves with many small leaflets are distinctive. Compare to chickpea (Cicer arietinum) but much smaller and more delicate.

Morphology आकारिकी

Habit: Erect or decumbent (spreading from base) annual or short-lived perennial herb; 30–80 cm tall; wiry, not woody; entire plant soft-hairy (pubescent); stems angular, greenish or reddish-tinged.

Leaves: Alternate, pinnately compound, 10–15 cm long; leaflets 8–15 per leaf, linear-oblong, 1–2 cm × 3–5 mm, slightly hairy above and below, margin entire, apex acute, base truncate or rounded. Petiole 0.5–1 cm; rachis (central axis) hairy.

Inflorescence: Short axillary racemes, 2–4 cm long, arising from leaf axils. Buds pink; flowers open progressively.

Flowers: Regular, 5-petalled; calyx 5-lobed, hairy; corolla pink to purple (sometimes pale), pea-like (papilionaceous); standard petal slightly larger and hooded; wings and keel smaller; 10 stamens (9 fused, 1 free); 1 pistil.

Fruits (legumes/pods): Linear, hairy, 4–6 cm long, 0.5–0.8 cm wide; slightly flattened; initially green, becoming grey-brown at maturity; opening along two sutures. Each pod contains 4–6 small, brownish-grey seeds, slightly flattened, oval.

Root system: A tap-root with several lateral branches; nodules visible on roots and lower stem, housing nitrogen-fixing symbiotic bacteria (Rhizobium species).

Behaviour & Ecology व्यवहार और पारिस्थितिकी

Tephrosia purpurea is a warmth-loving, drought-tolerant annual or short-lived perennial that thrives in disturbed, dry soils throughout the tropical and subtropical regions. It is a pioneer of waste ground, fallow fields, and roadside margins. The species is nitrogen-fixing due to symbiotic bacteria in root nodules, and it can improve soil fertility in depleted, sandy, or rocky soils. Once established, it competes vigorously with other small herbaceous plants but does not shade out larger vegetation.

The small flowers are visited by small bees, solitary wasps, and other minute insects for pollen and possibly nectar, making it a minor pollinator plant. Pollination is primarily by insects.

The seeds are not readily eaten by livestock, partly due to the presence of rotenone and other alkaloid-like compounds in the foliage and seeds that have insecticidal properties. This chemical defence is exploited in traditional pest control.

The plant establishes readily in sandy and rocky soils, helping to stabilize erosion. In traditional agriculture, it was sometimes left standing in the margins of fields as a natural pesticide and nitrogen fixer.

Life Cycle / Phenology जीवन चक्र

  • Germination: June–July (monsoon onset) or February–March (spring); rapid under warm, moist conditions
  • Vegetative growth: July–August; plant reaches full height by early monsoon
  • Flowering: July–September; flowers produced progressively on multiple racemes
  • Pod development: August–October; pods mature and dry on the plant
  • Seed dispersal: September–November; mature pods split; seeds fall near parent or are carried by wind and animals
  • Death: Plant may overwinter in mild conditions but typically dies by December–January cold in eastern UP
  • Persistence: Seeds remain viable in soil 2–3 years; can volunteer in fallows

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि और मानव संबंध

Traditional use: Tephrosia purpurea has been valued in South Asian agriculture and traditional medicine for over 2,000 years. Uses include:

  • Natural pesticide (rotenone source): The plant contains alkaloids including rotenone, a naturally occurring insecticide. Seeds were crushed and mixed with water or oil, then applied to crops or poured into ponds to stun or kill fish and insects. The compound rotenone has low mammalian toxicity and breaks down rapidly in sunlight, making it safer than synthetic pesticides. Traditional fishermen used it to catch fish by stupefaction rather than poison.
  • Medicinal uses in traditional systems: Root decoction traditionally given for skin diseases, eczema, and psoriasis. Leaf paste applied to wounds and insect bites. The plant is listed in Ayurvedic pharmacopeias as Sharpunkha for skin conditions and joint pain. These are traditional uses; no modern clinical validation is available, and self-treatment is not advised.
  • Nitrogen fixation: The plant was sometimes left in field margins or fallows to enrich soil before replanting.
  • Dye source: The roots and aerial parts contain indigo-like compounds; traditionally used to produce a reddish-purple dye, particularly for cloth.

Today: The plant is valued in organic farming as a green manure crop (nitrogen-fixing cover crop) in some regions. It is still used as a traditional pesticide in smallholder and organic systems in parts of India. The practice of using plant-based rotenone has declined in commercial agriculture due to synthetic alternatives, but it persists in organic and traditional farming communities.

Research direction (a lead, not a result): The rotenone content and extractability from Indian wild populations remain understudied. Quantifying the insecticidal efficacy against specific eastern UP pests (particularly rice and cotton pests) could inform organic agriculture recommendations. The nitrogen-fixing capacity in eastern UP soils, and its enhancement of crop growth in subsequent plantings, merits field trials. Modern phytochemical analysis of the root compounds — beyond rotenone — could identify other bioactive alkaloids with potential agricultural or pharmaceutical uses.

Caution - Not for self-medication: While traditionally used for skin conditions, no clinical evidence supports efficacy or safety. Rotenone, while less toxic than synthetic pesticides, is still an insecticide; ingestion or prolonged skin contact should be avoided. Always consult a healthcare provider before using plant-derived remedies.

Tephrosia purpurea is to be expected year-round on roadsides, field margins, waste ground, and fallow fields throughout Basti and eastern Uttar Pradesh, with peak presence and flowering July through September. Look in sandy or rocky soils, particularly in dry areas and along unwatered field margins. The feathery compound leaves and small pink-purple pea-like flowers are diagnostic. The first local record would be a photograph with date and location, sent to the atlas.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Native to tropical Asia, particularly the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia; now widely distributed across tropical regions.

In India: Present throughout the country from sea level to about 2,000 m elevation; particularly common in the drier plains and semi-arid regions.

In Uttar Pradesh: Common across all districts of the state, on roadsides, in wastelands, and fallow fields.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Expected as a common roadside and waste-ground weed; abundance increases on sandy, rocky, or well-drained soils. Exact local distribution awaits field surveys.

Research Questions शोध प्रश्न

Anyone can investigate these | कोई भी इन प्रश्नों की जाँच कर सकता है

  1. How much nitrogen does one plant fix in a season? / एक पौधा एक मौसम में कितनी नाइट्रोजन बाँधता है? — Harvest a mature plant; dry it and send leaf and root samples to a soil laboratory for total nitrogen analysis. Compare to a non-nitrogen-fixing plant from the same soil.
  2. Does rotenone from local plants actually kill mosquito larvae? / क्या इसके बीजों से बनी दवा दिनों को मारती है? — Crush fresh seeds; make a water extract; test on mosquito (Culex) larvae in controlled containers.
  3. What is the traditional use in your village or nearby? / आपके क्षेत्र में इसका क्या उपयोग है? — Interview 5 elders; document any use in farming or traditional medicine.
  4. Does soil amended with sarphonka support higher crop yields? / क्या सरफोंका वाली मिट्टी में फसल ज़्यादा उपज देती है? — Grow two plots of the same crop: one with sarphonka as green manure, one without; measure grain or vegetable yield.
  5. Can seed viability be extended by storage method? / बीजों को कैसे रखने से लंबे समय तक ताज़ा रहते हैं? — Store seeds in dry, cool conditions vs. ambient conditions; test germination after 6, 12, and 24 months.

Field rule: Low erect herb + feathery compound leaves (8–15 leaflets per leaf) + pink-purple pea-like flowers in short racemes + hairy linear pods = Tephrosia purpurea. The nitrogen-fixing nodules on the roots confirm the identification if you dig carefully.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. Tephrosia purpurea was first described in the species Galega purpurea by Carl Linnaeus in 1753 (Species Plantarum 1059). It was later transferred to the genus Tephrosia by Jean Louis Marie Poiret in 1808. The genus name Tephrosia is derived from the Greek tephros (ash-coloured), referring to the greyish hue of many species' flowers or foliage. The specific epithet purpurea refers to the purple colour of the flowers.

Subspecies. Monotypic — no subspecies are currently recognised.

Family and order placement. Belongs to the order Fabales, family Fabaceae (legumes or pea family), subfamily Faboideae. The family Fabaceae is one of the largest plant families, containing about 730 genera and 20,000 species globally. Tephrosia itself comprises about 400 species, mostly tropical and subtropical.

Phylogenetic notes. Tephrosia is part of the phaseoloid clade within Fabaceae, related to genera like Crotalaria, Indigofera, and Phaseolus. Molecular studies support Tephrosia as a well-defined genus within the subfamily.

IUCN status rationale. This species has not been formally assessed by the IUCN Red List. As a widespread, common weed with no conservation threats and stable populations, formal assessment would likely yield a status of Least Concern or Not Evaluated.
