नकटा / Knob-billed Duck

Sarkidiornis melanotos (Pennant, 1769) — Anatidae

नकटा — एक बड़ी, गहरे रंग की हंस, जिसके सिर पर एक बड़ी, काली गाँठ होती है — यही इसका सबसे विशेष लक्षण है और "knob-billed" या "नकटा" नाम से जाना जाता है। यह हंस पूर्वी उत्तर प्रदेश में मानसून और उसके बाद अनियमित रूप से दिखती है। नर बहुत बड़े होते हैं, मादा छोटी। ये बड़े झुंड में घास खाने के लिए भूमि पर उतरते हैं।

Field tip: The Knob-billed Duck is unmistakable — it is the largest duck in the region with a prominent black knob on the forehead (males). Look for large, dark ducks on the ground grazing in flocks during monsoon and post-monsoon months.

Size: Males 61–76 cm long; females 55–61 cm long. The largest duck in eastern UP. Wingspan 100–130 cm. Highly sexually dimorphic in size.

Plumage (male): Predominantly dark glossy black-green on head, neck, and upper back. Breast and underparts pure white. Lower back and rump glossy black. Bill pale yellow with black tip. The most distinctive feature: a large, smooth, black knob on the forehead, more pronounced in older males.

Plumage (female): Much duller than male. Dark grey-brown above; white or buff underparts. Lacks the prominent knob or has only a small knob.

Bill: Large, pale yellow with black tip. Broadly spatulate, used for grazing.

Legs and feet: Long, pale yellow. Placed far forward on body, allowing efficient terrestrial locomotion.

Eyes: Dark; positioned to look downward while grazing.

Sexes: Highly dimorphic. Males much larger (nearly twice the weight of females) and far more brightly coloured. Knob is the most obvious sexual character.

Juveniles: Resemble females but duller.

Vocalisations. Largely silent. Occasional calls:

  1. Flight call: Soft grunting sounds; frequency 0.5–1.5 kHz.
  2. Contact call: Quiet, occasional honks.
  3. Alarm: Sharp hissing if threatened at nest.

Behaviour & Ecology व्यवहार और पारिस्थितिकी

Grazing specialist: Knob-billed Ducks are unique among dabbling ducks in being highly terrestrial grazers. They feed on:

  • Grasses and grass seeds (primary diet)
  • Sedges and rushes
  • Aquatic plant shoots
  • Grain and crop residues
  • Aquatic invertebrates

Feeding technique: Spend much of the day on land, grazing in fields adjacent to water bodies. Eat grass directly by clipping with their broad bills. Occasional dabbling in shallow water. Never dive.

Social behaviour: Highly gregarious, especially outside breeding season. Form flocks of 20–100 or more birds. Males tend to congregate separately from females and young.

Habitat specialization: Requires both open water for roosting and adjacent open grassland or cultivated land for grazing.

Terrestrial strength: More at home on land than other ducks; walk with ease and spend hours grazing.

Flight: Heavy, slow fliers; strong takeoff from both water and land.

Predators: Golden Eagles, larger raptors, wild boar, jackals.

हिंदी: ज़मीन पर घास चरता है, पानी में कम रहता है। बड़े झुंड में रहता है।

Life Cycle / Phenology जीवन चक्र

Locally nomadic: Movements driven by water availability and grass growth. Present in eastern UP during monsoon (June–September) and post-monsoon (October–November) when water levels are high.

Breeding: June–September (monsoon). Nesting occurs in eastern UP during this period.

Nesting: Nest on ground in tall grass or reed beds; sometimes in trees. Simple nest of grass and down.

Eggs: 10–14 whitish eggs. Female alone incubates ~22–24 days.

Chicks: Precocial; fledge at ~8 weeks. Male does not participate in chick care.

Molting: Pre-breeding moult in May; post-breeding moult August–October.

Roosting: Communal night roosts on open water.

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि प्रासंगिकता

Protected species: Listed in Schedule IV of the Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972, amended 2022. Fully protected; hunting prohibited.

Grazing competition: Sometimes competes with livestock for grass in pastures and may cause minor damage to young crops by grazing, but overall impact is minimal due to localized and seasonal occurrence.

Cultural significance: Named नकटा (nakta) in Hindi, reference to the distinctive knob on the forehead. Appears in classical texts.

Hunting heritage: Historically hunted; no longer permitted in India.

Expected Locally स्थानीय रूप से अपेक्षित

This species is NOT a field record. Information below describes where Knob-billed Ducks should be looked for based on habitat and seasonal availability.

Seasonal presence: Expected in eastern UP during monsoon and post-monsoon (June–November), with peak visibility in September–October when water levels are optimal.

Habitat preference: Large lakes, floodplain lakes, and river margins adjacent to open grassland.

Irregular occurrence: Presence is highly irregular and depends on water availability and grass growth.

Nearest published record: The Ghat Lakes system near Balrampur and the Ghaghra floodplain have hosted breeding and wintering populations during wet years.

What a first record would be: A dated photograph clearly showing the large duck with or without the characteristic knob, with date and GPS location, submitted through the About page.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Africa and Indian subcontinent. Two subspecies; S. m. melanotos in Africa, S. m. ashtiana in India.

In India: Widespread but irregular; movements driven by monsoon.

In Uttar Pradesh: Eastern districts, particularly along major river systems.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Locally irregular during monsoon and post-monsoon on suitable wetlands and floodplains.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. Described by Thomas Pennant in 1769 in Indian Zoology, volume 1, as Anas melanotos. Type locality is India.

Subspecies. Two subspecies recognised:

  • S. m. melanotos Pennant, 1769 — Africa
  • S. m. ashtiana Neumann, 1910 — Indian subcontinent (occurs in eastern UP)

Family and order placement. Order Anseriformes; family Anatidae. Genus Sarkidiornis is monotypic (single species).

Phylogenetic notes. Recent molecular work suggests Sarkidiornis is distinct within Anatidae, representing a unique ecological niche as a grazing waterfowl.

IUCN status rationale. Least Concern globally. Populations stable in Africa and India. Protected under Schedule IV of Wildlife Protection Act 1972 (amended 2022) in India. Not threatened.
