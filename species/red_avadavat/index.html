लाल मुनिया / Red Avadavat

Amandava amandava (Linnaeus, 1758) — Estrildidae

लाल मुनिया — भारत की सबसे छोटी और सबसे रंगीन चिड़ियों में से एक है। नर पक्षी का पूरा शरीर लाल होता है, जबकि मादा भूरी होती है। ये चिड़िया नरसलों और सरकंडों के बीच घूमती हैं और कीड़े खोजती हैं। छोटे झुंडों में रहती है, खासकर सर्दियों में। इसकी आवाज़ बहुत ही कमजोर और मधुर होती है। गाँवों के पास तालाबों और जलाशयों में ये आसानी से देखी जा सकती हैं।

Field tip: Look for small flocks in reed beds, grasslands, and areas near water. The breeding male is brilliant red and unmistakable. Females and non-breeding birds are buff-coloured and require care to identify. Listen for soft, thin, squeaky calls given by flocks. Often seen perched on reed stems or grass, gleaning seeds.

Plumage breeding male: Head, neck, back, rump, and wings bright crimson or scarlet red. Lores, cheeks, and chin red. Breast, belly, and undertail coverts red with fine black spotting (spots on breast and belly; spotting density varies with age and breeding condition). Wing coverts and flight feathers red with black edges or markings. Tail short, graduated; red with black central edges.

Plumage non-breeding male and female: Overall buff, buff-brown, or pale rufous. Crown and back buff-brown; underparts pale buff or whitish. Wings and tail duller, pale brown. Little or no red in plumage. Some individuals may show faint red tinge on rump or breast.

Bare parts: Eyes small, dark brown. Bill short, conical, stout for cracking seeds; bright red or pinkish-red in breeding season, duller horn-coloured in non-breeding.

Legs and feet: Short, reddish-brown or dark.

Sexes and seasonal variation: Breeding males are brilliant red; females and non-breeding males are buff to buff-brown. Variation in brightness correlates with breeding status and age.

Juveniles: Similar to females; duller; lack red colouration.

Vocalisations

Red Avadavat is relatively quiet but produces soft, thin calls within flocks.

  1. Contact call: Soft, thin, squeaky "zee" or "it," uttered frequently while flocking or feeding.
  2. Flock call: Soft twittering or chippy sounds when flocks are foraging together.
  3. Song: Males produce a very soft, gentle warbling song, rarely heard; delivered from reed stems or low perches.

Behaviour & Ecology व्यवहार और पारिस्थितिकी

Specialized grass-seed eater: Red Avadavat is a specialist feeder on small grass seeds, particularly seeds of reed beds and waterside grasses. Forages by clinging to grass stems and seed heads, gleaning seeds with its stout, pointed bill.

Gregarious and flock-living: Always found in flocks, from small groups to flocks of 20+ birds. Flocks feed, roost, and move together with constant soft vocalisation maintaining contact.

Water-dependent: Requires regular access to water for drinking and bathing. Usually found within 100–200 metres of water sources (ponds, river margins, wetlands).

Breeding in flocks: Unlike most estrildid finches that pair off, Red Avadavat maintains some gregariousness even during breeding season, with multiple nests in close proximity (loose colonies).

Breeding and nesting: Monogamous. Builds globular or oval-shaped nest of grass and plant material, interwoven with fine twigs and fibres. Nest is suspended from reed stem or bush over water, typically at 1–3 metres height. Clutch size 4–6 eggs; incubation 11–13 days; fledging 12–15 days.

Life Cycle / Phenology जीवन चक्र

  • March–April: Males acquire breeding plumage (moult into red); territories begin to form
  • April–May: Pair formation; nesting material collection; nest building begins
  • May–July: Egg-laying and incubation (peak breeding May-June)
  • June–August: Chick-rearing; fledging occurs; pairs may attempt second broods
  • August–September: Post-breeding; flocks form; reduced breeding activity
  • September–November: Moult into non-breeding plumage (males lose red, become buff)
  • November–March: Non-breeding season; large flocks roost together; males duller

Host Plants or Prey आहार

Specialized seed diet:

  • Grass seeds (particularly reed and waterside grasses): Phragmites, Acanthus, Sacciolepis, Paspalum
  • Small weed seeds
  • Very occasionally insects (when feeding chicks)
  • Plant matter and seeds from cultivated and wild grasses

Predators / Natural Enemies शत्रु

  • Shikra (Accipiter badius) and other small raptors — hunt flying flocks
  • Hobby (Falco subbuteo) and other falcons — hunt in flight
  • Snakes — raid nests; consume eggs and chicks
  • Crows and mynas — harass flocks; may predate chicks
  • Domestic cats — hunt at water sources and roosting sites

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि प्रासंगिकता

Weed seed control: By consuming grass and weed seeds, Red Avadavat may contribute to natural weed control in wetlands and grasslands, potentially benefiting agricultural areas adjacent to wetlands.

Cultural significance: In rural India, the Red Avadavat is sometimes kept in captivity as a cage bird due to its bright colouration and small size. Though protection laws forbid this, illegal trapping still occurs in some regions. The species is part of traditional bird culture in India.

Protected status: Schedule IV of the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 (India). The species is legally protected throughout the country.

Expected Locally स्थानीय रूप से अपेक्षित

Not a field record. Written from published accounts for eastern Uttar Pradesh. No confirmed local sighting yet — if you have seen it, that is what the atlas needs.
अभी तक स्थानीय पुष्टि नहीं। यह विवरण प्रकाशित स्रोतों से है, किसी अवलोकन से नहीं।

Red Avadavat is expected year-round as a resident in Basti and Ayodhya districts, particularly in wetlands, reed beds, and grasslands near water sources. Look for small flocks in reed beds and on grass seed heads, especially near ponds, river margins, and ditches. Breeding males in bright red plumage are unmistakable during May-July; non-breeding birds and females are buff-coloured and more subtle. Listen for soft, thin calls given by flocking birds.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Widespread across the Indian subcontinent, with populations also occurring in Pakistan, Bangladesh, Myanmar, and other parts of South and Southeast Asia.

In India: Common resident throughout the mainland in suitable wetland and grassland habitats, from sea level to 1,500 metres.

In Uttar Pradesh: Resident throughout the state in areas with wetlands and grasslands.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Expected as a resident in wetlands, reed beds, and grasslands, particularly near the Ghaghra river system and associated water bodies.

Field rule: Very small finch + brilliant red breeding male or buff female + flock living + near water + grass seed feeding + soft "zee" calls = Red Avadavat. The breeding male's bright red plumage is unmistakable.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. This species was first described as Fringilla amandava by Carl Linnaeus in 1758 in Systema Naturae. The genus Amandava was erected later to contain this and related species. The scientific name refers to Ahmadnagar (Amandabad), a city in Maharashtra, India, where the bird was noted.

Subspecies. Two subspecies recognised: A. a. amandava (nominate, India and this subspecies in UP) and A. a. flavidiventris (Southeast Asian populations). The Indian subspecies is brighter and more strongly marked than the Southeast Asian form.

Family and order placement. Order Passeriformes; family Estrildidae (estrildid finches or waxbills). Estrildidae includes about 140 species of small, colourful finches, primarily from Africa, Asia, and Australia. Members of this family are popular in the cage-bird trade.

Phylogenetic notes. Molecular studies place Amandava within a clade of Asian estrildid finches. The genus is closely related to other Asian and African waxbills.

IUCN status rationale. Assessed as Least Concern (LC). The species is widespread, maintains stable populations, and shows flexibility in habitat use across its range, despite some illegal trapping for the cage-bird trade.
