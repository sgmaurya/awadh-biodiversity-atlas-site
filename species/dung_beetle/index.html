गोबर भृंग / Giant Dung Beetle

Catharsius molossus (Linnaeus, 1758) — Scarabaeidae

गोबर भृंग — ग्रामीण क्षेत्रों में पशुचारण के इलाकों में पाया जाने वाला एक बड़ा काला भृंग जो पशुओं के गोबर को दफनाता है। यह अपनी मजबूत और खोदाई के लिए बने पैरों से मिट्टी में सुरंगें खोदता है और गोबर की गेंदें बनाकर उन्हें नीचे दफनाता है। इस काम से गाँव के मैदानों में मक्खियाँ और बीमारियाँ नहीं फैलती, और मिट्टी भी अच्छी रहती है।

Field tip: Look for a medium-sized, glossy black beetle near fresh cattle dung in pastures, especially during monsoon. The shovel-like head and robust digging legs are distinctive. Males have a small curved horn on the head. Active at dusk and night, often seen flying near lights.

हिंदी — कैसे पहचानें: मध्यम आकार का चमकदार काला भृंग। गोबर के पास मिलता है। नर के सिर पर छोटा सींग होता है।

Morphology आकारिकी

Size. About 25–35 mm, males up to about 45 mm, as given in summary accounts; the measured series is in Arrow (1931) and Pokorný et al. (2009).

Body structure: Robust, heavily sclerotized, and convex. The head is flat with a prominent clypeus (shovel-like anterior margin) for cutting and pushing dung. The body is more compact than the larger Heliocopris genus.

Sexual dimorphism:

  • Males: Bear a curved, upright horn on the head (epicranial horn); pronotum is sculptured with lateral margins prominent.
  • Females: Lack the prominent horn; head has a small transverse ridge; pronotum is flatter.

Elytra: Hard forewings cover the entire abdomen, marked with shallow longitudinal striae (lines). Beneath are membranous hindwings enabling flight.

Legs: Specially adapted for digging. The tibia of the forelegs is broadened and armed with 3–4 large teeth on the outer margin, forming a spade-like structure for soil excavation.

Behaviour & Ecology व्यवहार और पारिस्थितिकी

Dung burial and tunnelling: Catharsius molossus is a tunneller (a paracoprid in the terms of Halffter & Edmonds 1982): it digs beneath or beside the fresh dung pad and packs dung into chambers below, where it is shaped into brood balls. It does not roll balls across the ground. Heliocopris works the same way on a larger scale.

Nutrient cycling: In eastern UP villages with abundant cattle, these beetles bury dung into the soil, transporting nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium into crop root zones. This reduces ammonia loss and enhances soil fertility naturally.

Fly and parasite suppression: By rapidly burying fresh dung, they eliminate breeding substrate for filth flies and interrupt the life cycle of gastrointestinal nematode parasites of livestock.

Soil aeration: Deep burrowing breaks hard clayey soil, improving aeration, porosity and water infiltration.

Life Cycle / Phenology जीवन चक्र

Stage / MonthEvent
April–MayAdults emerge from soil as temperatures rise; mate in dung piles
June–OctoberActive season — Monsoon rains; peak breeding and nesting; pairs dig deep tunnels under dung
July–SeptemberFemale deposits eggs in brood balls; larvae feed inside the balls
September–NovemberNew adults develop within soil; may overwinter or remain active
December–MarchReduced activity in dry season; adults remain in soil; some activity on warm days

Diet and Feeding Behaviour आहार

Coprophagous (dung-feeding) throughout life:

  • Primary food: Fresh cattle and buffalo dung; highly preferred
  • Secondary food: Sheep and goat dung; less preferred than bovine dung
  • Adult feeding: Consume dung during nesting and between breeding cycles
  • Larval feeding: Develop entirely within the brood ball, eating it from the inside

Predators / Natural Enemies शत्रु

  • Birds — hoopoes, crows and mynas at the dung pad and under lights
  • Mammals — the grey mongoose and rats digging into tunnels
  • Reptiles — large lizards
  • Mites — phoretic mites ride on the beetles between dung pads; they are passengers, not parasites

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि प्रासंगिकता

Dung management: In eastern UP farming villages, cattle dung accumulation in pastures and sheds is a persistent problem. These beetles function as natural sanitation workers, removing fresh dung and reducing disease transmission pathways.

Livestock health: By burying dung rapidly, they reduce fly populations and interrupt parasite life cycles, indirectly benefiting cattle health.

Chemical threat: Residues of ivermectin and related cattle dewormers pass into the dung and are toxic to dung-beetle larvae (Lumaret et al. 2012).

Soil health: Farmers benefit from improved soil structure, reduced compaction, and natural fertilization through buried dung without mechanical plowing.

GBIF holds a published record from Uttar Pradesh, none yet from the Awadh–Purvanchal plain or Basti–Ayodhya.

Catharsius molossus is expected year-round in rural Basti and Ayodhya, particularly in pastoral areas with cattle. Look for it near cattle sheds, village grazing lands, and livestock pens (gaushalas). Most visible during monsoon (June–October). A first record would be a photograph showing the glossy black body, shovel-like head, and digging posture, with date and location noted through the About page.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Widespread across tropical and subtropical South and Southeast Asia, including India, Nepal, Myanmar, Thailand, and Sri Lanka.

In India: Recorded across the country, from the Himalayan foothills to the south and the Andamans (Arrow 1931).

In Uttar Pradesh: GBIF holds a record from the state, none from the Awadh–Purvanchal plain.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Expected in all pastoral and agricultural areas with cattle.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. Linnaeus described the species as Scarabaeus molossus in 1758 in the Systema Naturae (10th edition, p. 351). The species was later transferred to the genus Catharsius. The epithet molossus is the name of the heavy Molossian hound of antiquity.

Until November 2026 this profile carried the name Heliocopris dominus, a species of Southeast Asia with no Uttar Pradesh record in GBIF; the profile was rewritten for the widespread Indian species, which has a published Uttar Pradesh record and none from the 33 districts under either name.

Subspecies. Monotypic — no subspecies are currently recognised.

Family and order placement. Order Coleoptera (beetles), family Scarabaeidae (scarab beetles), subfamily Scarabaeinae (true dung beetles), tribe Coprini. The genus Catharsius is closely related to Heliocopris but occupies a distinct ecological role with moderate-sized colonies.

Phylogenetic notes. Catharsius beetles are Old World coprophages. The genus is distributed across tropical Asia and Africa. Catharsius molossus is one of the most widespread and abundant dung beetles of tropical Asia (Arrow 1931).

IUCN status rationale. Not evaluated.
