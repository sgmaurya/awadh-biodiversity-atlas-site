पपीहा / Common Hawk-Cuckoo

Hierococcyx varius (Vahl, 1797) — Cuculidae

पपीहा — ग्रीष्म का चिड़िया, जिसकी तीखी और दोहरी आवाज़ "पीऊ-पीऊ" या "ब्रेन-फीवर, ब्रेन-फीवर" कहलाती है। यह अप्रैल में भारत में आता है और अक्तूबर तक रहता है। बारिश के दिनों में इसकी पुकार गाँवों और जंगलों में गूंजती है। हिंदी साहित्य में पपीहा उस बिछड़े हुए प्रेमी का प्रतीक है जो अपनी प्रिय को याद करके रोता है। कवि मलिक मुहम्मद जायसी और दूसरी भारतीय परंपराओं में पपीहा की तरह विरह (जुदाई) की आवाज़ सदियों से सुनी जाती है।

Plumage: Head and nape greyish-brown. Back, rump, and wing coverts uniform dark grey or grey-brown. Flight feathers darker grey or blackish-grey. Underparts mostly white; breast greyish-buff with grey barring; flanks and undertail coverts barred with grey. Tail graduated, grey, with white or pale tips to outer feathers (visible in flight).

Facial features: Orbital ring yellow or yellowish-green. Eye dark. Lores pale.

Bill: Greenish-yellow or pale greenish; moderate length; slightly downcurved.

Legs and feet: Pale greenish-grey or greenish-yellow.

Sexes: Similar; females may have slightly more barring on breast.

Juveniles: Resembles adults but duller; head and back more brownish; breast less clearly barred.

Vocalisations

Common Hawk-Cuckoo is celebrated for its loud and distinctive voice, which inspired its English name "Brainfever Bird."

  1. Advertising call: A loud, sharp, two-syllable "piu-piu" or "piu-WHIT," repeated in rapid series, rising in pitch and intensity. The call is often described phonetically as "brain-fever, brain-fever" due to its repetitive, maddening quality. This call is uttered from dawn to mid-morning and again late afternoon, most intensely in spring and early summer (April-June).
  2. Contact call: Softer, single-syllable "piu" or "chee."
  3. Alarm: Harsh, rapid chattering when disturbed.

Behaviour & Ecology व्यवहार और पारिस्थितिकी

Frugivore and seed-disperser: Common Hawk-Cuckoo feeds primarily on figs, berries, and other wild fruits, particularly favouring Ficus species. Moves actively through the canopy, gleaning fruit. Important disperser of fig seeds across its range.

Brood parasite: Like other cuckoos, this species is an obligate brood parasite. Primary hosts are babblers and other large passerines, particularly grey-breasted laughingthrush and other Garrulax species. Females locate nests, eject an egg, and lay one of their own.

Migratory: A true seasonal migrant, arriving in India with spring rains (March-April) and departing in autumn (October-November). Arrival is loosely synchronised with monsoon onset and the breeding cycle of host species.

Solitary and territorial: Males hold territories and call from exposed or semi-exposed perches. Females are less vocal and more secretive when prospecting for host nests.

Life Cycle / Phenology जीवन चक्र

  • March–April: Arrival in India; spring migration; males establish territories
  • April–May: Peak territorial calling; females scout for host nests; parasitism begins
  • May–July: Egg-laying in host nests; incubation of cuckoo eggs (12–14 days)
  • June–August: Cuckoo chicks hatch and grow in host nests (fledging at 20–25 days); host parents raise chicks
  • August–September: Fledglings become independent; some second broods may occur
  • September–October: Pre-departure; birds become quieter
  • October–November: Departure for wintering grounds
  • December–February: Absent from India

Host Plants or Prey आहार

Primary diet: Fruits and berries, particularly those of Ficus species.

  • Figs (Ficus religiosa, F. benghalensis, F. carica)
  • Berries of various wild shrubs
  • Drupes and wild fruits in season
  • Rarely insects (captured in flight)

Hosts for parasitism: Primarily babblers and laughingthrushes.

  • Grey-breasted Laughingthrush (Garrulax jerdoni)
  • Other babbler species (Turdoides and Argya spp.)

Predators / Natural Enemies शत्रु

  • Shikra (Accipiter badius) — hunts flying cuckoos
  • Grey-breasted Laughingthrush (Garrulax jerdoni) and other host species — may destroy cuckoo eggs or attack adults near nests
  • Snakes — raid nests; may consume eggs and chicks
  • Crows (Corvus spp.) — mob and harass; may take eggs if nest is discovered

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि प्रासंगिकता

Seed dispersal and fruit ecology: By consuming and dispersing fig seeds across the landscape, Common Hawk-Cuckoo contributes to forest regeneration and connectivity.

Cultural significance: The Common Hawk-Cuckoo holds a unique place in Indian literary and musical tradition, particularly in Hindi, Urdu, and regional literature, where it represents not just seasonal change but also human longing and separation.

Traditional literature: In medieval Hindi and Urdu poetry, the "पपीहा" (papiha) appears frequently as a symbol of longing and separation (विरह - viraha). The 16th-century poet Malik Muhammad Jayasi uses the papiha in Padmavat to represent a soul separated from the divine beloved, crying in separation. The cuckoo's call, like that of the Pied Cuckoo, symbolizes the monsoon, reunion with the beloved, and the pain of waiting.

Modern evocation: In Hindi film and popular culture, the papiha's call is often used to evoke the monsoon season and romantic longing. The bird's arrival marks the beginning of the monsoon and the rainy season's emotional associations.

Protected status: Schedule IV of the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 (India). The species is legally protected throughout the country.

Common Hawk-Cuckoo is expected as a summer visitor (March-October) in Basti and Ayodhya districts, particularly in areas with fruit-bearing trees (mango groves, orchards) and mixed deciduous forests. Listen for the loud, distinctive "piu-piu" or "brain-fever" call from March onwards, especially in early morning and late afternoon, from April to July when calling is most intense. The arrival of the first calls (mid-March to early April) heralds spring and the approach of the monsoon.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Seasonal migrant across South Asia — breeds across northern and central India (March-October), winters in peninsular India and Sri Lanka.

In India: Summer and post-monsoon visitor to most of the mainland, particularly in areas with suitable forest and fruit-bearing trees. Absent from the driest deserts and highest mountains.

In Uttar Pradesh: Summer visitor to most districts where mixed deciduous forest and mango groves occur.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Expected as a summer visitor (March-October) in fruit-bearing trees, forest edges, and degraded mixed forest adjacent to cultivation.

Field rule: Medium grey and white cuckoo + loud two-syllable "piu-piu" or "brain-fever" call rising in pitch + spring arrival + fruit-eating = Common Hawk-Cuckoo. The call is unmistakable and diagnostic.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. This species was originally described as Cuculus varius by Martin Vahl in 1797, based on a specimen from India (exact locality not specified, but likely the Indian plains).

Subspecies. Two subspecies recognised: H. v. varius (nominate, occurring in India and the subspecies in eastern UP) and H. v. nisicolor (breeding in northern limits, winter visitor to parts of India). The nominate form is larger and darker than the northern subspecies.

Family and order placement. Order Cuculiformes; family Cuculidae (cuckoos). The genus Hierococcyx includes aggressive brood parasites that may lay multiple eggs in a single host nest.

Phylogenetic notes. Molecular studies place Hierococcyx as closely related to Asian cuckoos; its hawk-like appearance is convergent.

IUCN status rationale. Assessed as Least Concern (LC). The species is widespread across its breeding range and maintains stable populations despite habitat changes.
