मीठे पानी का घोंघा / Freshwater Pond Snail

Lymnaea acuminata Brongniart, 1810 — Lymnaeidae

मीठे पानी का घोंघा — पूर्वी उत्तर प्रदेश के तालाबों, पोखरों और सिंचित धान के खेतों में पाया जाने वाला एक छोटा, नाजुक और शंकुधारी मीठे पानी का घोंघा। इसका खोल पारदर्शी, हल्का पीला या भूरा होता है और ऊपर की ओर तेजी से नुकीला (acuminate) होता जाता है। यह पानी में तैरते हुए शैवाल और सड़ने वाले पौधों को खाता है। हालांकि यह जलीय पारिस्थितिकी तंत्र की सफाई में मदद करता है, लेकिन कृषि और पशुपालन के दृष्टिकोण से यह अत्यधिक महत्वपूर्ण है क्योंकि यह पशुओं में 'लिवर फ्लूक' (Fasciola gigantica) बीमारी फैलाने वाले परजीवी का मुख्य मध्यवर्ती मेजबान (intermediate host) है।

Quick Identification त्वरित पहचान

FeatureDescription
Shell FormThin, fragile, ovate-oblong shell with a very large body whorl and a short, highly pointed spire
Shell ColorPale yellowish-horn to translucent brownish-grey; surface is relatively smooth and shiny
ApertureExceptionally large, oval opening (aperture), occupying more than three-quarters of the total shell height
OperculumAbsent (unlike Apple Snails); retracts directly into the shell without a closing lid
BodySoft, pale greyish-green or yellowish body with broad, flat, triangular tentacles
MovementSlow gliding over aquatic plants, mud surfaces, or floating upside down on the water film

Field tip: Look for a small, thin, pointed snail (under 3 cm) with an extremely large opening that takes up most of its shell. Commonly found crawling on the undersides of floating lotus or water lily leaves, or on submerged stems in stagnant village ponds.

Morphology आकारिकी

MeasurementRange
Shell length15–30 mm
Shell width8–15 mm
Weight0.5–3 g

Shell structure: The shell is dextral (right-handed whorls), thin, delicate, and translucent. It features a very large, inflated body whorl that rapidly expands towards the opening (aperture), while the upper spire is short, narrow, and sharply pointed (acuminate), which gives the species its scientific name.

Aperture and lack of operculum: The aperture is wide, outer lip thin and sharp. There is no operculum (sealing lid); the animal relies on retreating deep into its shell and secreting mucus to prevent drying out if exposed to air.

Body parts: The head carries a pair of broad, flat, triangular tentacles with eyes situated at their inner bases. The muscular foot is relatively broad and short, allowing the snail to crawl on soft mud or hang from the surface film of water.

Respiratory system: Pulmonary. It has a lung-like respiratory cavity (pulmonary sac) and must periodically rise to the surface of stagnant water to breathe air.

Ecological Role पारिस्थितिक भूमिका

Benthic grazer and detritivore. Lymnaea acuminata feeds primarily by scraping off micro-algae, diatoms, and biofilms from submerged stones and plant surfaces using its chitinous tongue-like radula. It also consumes decaying organic matter, helping recycle organic nutrients in the pond ecosystem.

Intermediate host of Fasciola liver fluke. This is the species' most ecologically and agriculturally significant role. It serves as the obligatory intermediate host for the digenetic trematode Fasciola gigantica (giant liver fluke), a parasite that infects the liver and bile ducts of domestic ruminants (cattle, buffaloes, sheep, and goats).

  • The parasite's larval stages (miracidia) penetrate the snail's body.
  • Inside the snail, they multiply and develop into sporocysts, rediae, and eventually free-swimming cercariae.
  • The cercariae emerge from the snail and encyst on aquatic vegetation (like water spinach or water chestnut) as metacercariae, which are then consumed by livestock grazing near the water.

Life Cycle / Phenology जीवन चक्र

  • Reproduction: Hermaphroditic (possessing both male and female reproductive organs), but cross-fertilization is typical.
  • Egg laying: Eggs are deposited in elongated, gelatinous ribbons or capsules attached to the underside of floating leaves, aquatic vegetation, or stones. Each capsule contains 20 to 150 small, transparent eggs.
  • Development: Direct development occurs within the egg capsule, with no free-swimming larval stage. Tiny juvenile snails (about 1.5–2 mm) hatch in 10 to 15 days, immediately beginning to feed on biofilms.
  • Seasonality: Breeding occurs year-round but peaks during the monsoon and post-monsoon months (July–November) when water bodies are full and vegetation is abundant, leading to massive population explosions.

Host Plants or Prey आहार

Dietary preferences:

  • Submerged micro-algae and green biofilms.
  • Decomposing leaves of aquatic plants (such as lotus, water chestnut, and water hyacinth).
  • Submerged organic detritus on the muddy bottom.

Predators / Natural Enemies शत्रु

  • Birds — Indian Pond Heron (BRD-0004), White Wagtail (BRD-0015), and ducks.
  • Fish — Carnivorous mud fishes like Snakehead Murrel (FSH-0003) and Walking Catfish (FSH-0004).
  • Invertebrates — Predatory water beetles and their larvae.

Agricultural / Human Relevance कृषि प्रासंगिकता

Veterinary impact in Basti. In the agricultural landscape of Basti, livestock farming is a major economic activity. The high prevalence of Lymnaea acuminata in village ponds and flooded paddy fields directly correlates with high rates of fascioliasis (liver fluke disease) in cows and buffaloes. Infected animals suffer from severe anemia, weight loss, drop in milk yield, liver damage, and death, causing major economic losses to farmers.

Management and control. Traditional methods to control liver fluke transmission involve managing snail populations. This includes introducing snail-eating fish, clearing excessive floating weeds (which serve as egg-laying substrates), and avoiding grazing livestock in wet pond margins during peak monsoon seasons.

Traditional medicine. In some rural areas of eastern UP, the crushed shells of these snails are traditionally burnt and mixed with mustard oil to make a paste used for treating minor skin infections in livestock.

Abundant in village ponds (pokharis), sluggish irrigation canals, and waterlogged rice fields throughout Basti district. During the monsoon, they are easily collected by lifting floating water lily leaves. Farmers are highly aware of the link between these small snails and liver rot in cattle, often referring to mud-pastures with high snail counts as "sick lands" (kasht wali zamin) during wet months.

Distribution वितरण

Global range: Distributed across South Asia, primarily in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Myanmar, and Nepal.

In India: Widespread in the freshwater systems of the Gangetic plains and central India.

In Uttar Pradesh: Common and resident in all districts with stagnant water bodies and agricultural irrigation systems.

In Basti district / eastern UP: Highly common resident in all low-lying wetlands and agricultural ponds.

Range notes: No significant range changes documented.

Taxonomy & Classification वर्गीकरण

Original description. Alexandre Brongniart described the species as Lymnaea acuminata in 1810 in the Annales du Muséum d'Histoire Naturelle (Vol. 15, p. 372). The type locality is India. The genus name Lymnaea is derived from the Greek limne (pool or marsh). The specific epithet acuminata is Latin for pointed or sharpened, directly referring to the acute spire.

Subspecies. Monotypic — no subspecies are currently recognised. Some researchers treat it under the genus Radix as Radix acuminata.

Family and order placement. Placed in the order Lymnaeida, family Lymnaeidae (pond snails). Lymnaeidae is a family of air-breathing freshwater snails characterized by dextral shells and broad, flat tentacles.

Phylogenetic notes. Lymnaea acuminata is closely related to Radix auricularia (Ear Pond Snail). Taxonomic classification remains dynamic, with some authors placing it in the genus Radix based on molecular and anatomical structures of the reproductive system.

IUCN status rationale. Not assessed by the IUCN Red List (Not Evaluated). Widespread and common, but locally managed as a vector of veterinary disease.
