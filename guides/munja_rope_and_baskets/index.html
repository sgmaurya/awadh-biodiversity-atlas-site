मूँज: रस्सी, टोकरी और चटाईMunja: rope, baskets and mats

Traditional craft · GDE-0001 · written 2026-10-21

Fibre and rope रेशा और रस्सीBaskets, mats and thatch टोकरी, चटाई, छप्पर

The tall grass on every bund and railway bank was rope, charpai webbing, baskets and screens in every house a generation ago. What is documented about the craft, who still does it, and what a self-help group would need to know.

What munja is मूँज क्या है

Munja Grass (Saccharum bengalense, also written S. munja) is the tall, tufted grass of embankments, railway banks, river sand and field bunds across the Gangetic plain. It is often confused with Kans Grass (Saccharum spontaneum), which is shorter, spreads by runners and invades fallow fields; munja stays in clumps where it is planted or left.

Two different materials come from the same plant, and the words matter:

  • Sarkanda (सरकंडा) is the flowering culm, the stiff reed two to four metres tall that

carries the silvery plume in October. It is used whole or split: for screens (chik), thatch, fences, and the frames of stools and chairs.

  • Munj (मूँज) is the leaf-sheath that wraps the upper culm. Stripped off and dried, it is

a long, pale, tough fibre. This is the rope and basket material.

The lower leaves (kaas, काँस, not to be confused with the kans grass species) are coarser and go into thatch, coarse mats and the cores of coiled baskets.

Traditional (documented) परंपरा में

Watt's Dictionary of the Economic Products of India (1893) and The Wealth of India describe munja as one of the principal cordage fibres of northern India. The documented uses, in rough order of how much fibre they consumed:

  1. Rope (ban, बान; munj ki rassi). Twisted by hand or on a simple spindle, two- and

three-strand. Used for tying bundles and loads, binding thatch, tethering cattle, well ropes, and above all for the webbing of the charpai, the string cot. A charpai woven with munj ban was the normal village bed until nylon tape replaced it in the 1980s and 1990s.

  1. Coiled baskets (dauri, pitari, chhabri, tokri). A core bundle of kaas, kans

or sarkanda strips is wound in a spiral; each turn is bound to the one below with a wrapped strip of wetted munj, pushed through with an awl (sua). The result is stiff, light and surprisingly long-lived: grain bins, bread baskets, lidded boxes for clothes, trays for winnowing and drying.

  1. Mats and screens. Sarkanda stems laid side by side and bound with munj cord make the

chik (rolled screen) and the tatti (fixed screen) hung in doorways against sun and dust; the same technique makes fences and the walls of cattle sheds.

  1. Thatch. Kaas leaves and spent culms as roofing for sheds and temporary shelters.
  2. The sacred girdle. A munj cord (muñjamekhalā) is tied round the waist of the boy

at the upanayana ceremony in some traditions; the grass is named for it in the old texts, which is why it is in the atlas's sacred-and-cultural list.

Who did it: in eastern UP the craft was part of ordinary household work rather than a caste trade, with rope-twisting done by men and coiled basketry largely by women in the winter months after the harvest, when the fibre had been cut and dried.

Today आज

  • Prayagraj's "moonj" craft. Uttar Pradesh's One District One Product scheme lists

moonj products as the craft of Prayagraj district; the villages around Naini and Mahewa make coiled baskets, trays, planters, lamp shades and furniture for urban and export markets, with design input from the state's craft programmes. It is the nearest living model of the craft, about 200 km from Basti.

  • Sarkanda furniture. Stools (moodha), chairs and screens of sarkanda remain a

roadside trade in Punjab, Haryana and western UP.

  • In Basti and Ayodhya, the plant is everywhere and the rope is nearly gone. Charpais are

woven with nylon tape; bundles are tied with plastic twine. The remaining knowledge sits with people now in their sixties and seventies. Recording it is urgent in a way that recording the plant is not.

How it is done, as the sources describe it तरीका, जैसा स्रोत बताते हैं

Read this as a description, not as instructions tested by the project.

Harvest. The culms are cut after flowering, from late October through December, when the sheath has matured but before the winter dries it brittle. Cutting earlier gives weak fibre; cutting the clump to the ground every year is what keeps it productive.

Stripping and drying. The sheath is peeled from the culm by hand, sorted by length, and dried in the sun for several days. Dried munj stores for a year or more if kept dry.

Softening. Before use the dried strips are soaked in water for some hours, or sprinkled and wrapped overnight, until they bend without cracking. Some makers beat the wetted strips lightly with a wooden mallet.

Rope. Two bundles of wetted strips are twisted in the same direction (each becoming a strand), then the strands are laid together twisting the opposite way; new strips are fed in as the old ones run out, so the rope can be any length. A three-strand ban for charpai webbing is about the thickness of a finger.

Coiled basket. A thin bundle of core material is started as a tight spiral; a wetted munj strip wraps the bundle and, every turn, passes through the coil below with an awl, locking the layers. The wall is shaped by moving the coil inward or outward. Dyed strips (today mostly with synthetic dyes; traditionally with Palash flower, turmeric and indigo) make the patterns. A lid is a second, shallower spiral.

Finishing. Dried in shade, trimmed, and sometimes rubbed with a little mustard oil.

Research direction (a lead, not a result) शोध की दिशा

  • Published laboratory studies test Saccharum munja fibre as reinforcement in polymer

composites and as a pulp for paper and board. Nothing at village scale has been reported.

  • The agronomy is simpler than for most craft plants: munja needs no irrigation or input,

stabilises bunds and canal banks, and can be cut annually. Whether a Basti self-help group can make a living from it depends on design and market, not on the plant.

Safety सावधानी

The dry leaf margins are sharp and cut hands; sarkanda stubble pierces bare feet. The dust of dry munj is an irritant when stripping in quantity. Nothing about the plant is toxic.

What we do not know, and how to help हम क्या नहीं जानते

  • Who in Basti or Ayodhya still twists ban or coils munj baskets, and the local words for

the tools and the products. A conversation with one maker, written down with their permission and a photograph of their hands at work, would be the first field record of this craft for the atlas.

  • Where the plant is cut today, by whom, and whether any is sold.
  • Whether the Prayagraj cluster buys raw fibre from outside its district.
