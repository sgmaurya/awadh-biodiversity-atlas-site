Three ways to wash with local plants, from the one that needs no chemistry to the one that needs care with lye. What is traditional claim, what is tested, and what is not safe.

Three different things called "soap" तीन अलग चीज़ें

  1. Saponin washes. Soapnut fruit and Shikakai pods contain saponins: molecules

with a water-loving sugar end and a fat-loving end, which is exactly what a detergent is. Soak or boil them and the water lathers and cleans. No chemistry is done; the plant made the detergent. This is what northern India washed wool, silk, jewellery and hair with for centuries, and it is the safe place to start.

  1. Hair-wash powders. Dried reetha, shikakai and Indian Gooseberry ground together: the saponins

clean, the amla's acids and tannins condition. A living trade in every bazaar.

  1. Real soap. Fat plus alkali, which is to say Neem oil, coconut oil or any other

oil reacted with sodium hydroxide (caustic soda, lye). This is saponification: the alkali splits each fat molecule into glycerol and three fatty-acid salts, and the salts are soap. It makes a bar, it keeps, it can be sold. It also involves a chemical that blinds and burns if mishandled, so it is the last of the three to try.

Reetha and shikakai: what is documented रीठा और शिकाकाई

Kirtikar & Basu, Chopra's Glossary and The Wealth of India record the dried pericarp of

Sapindus mukorossi and the pods of Senegalia rugata as the standard washing materials of the subcontinent for fine cloth, metal ornaments and hair; both also appear in those works as traditional remedies (emetic, expectorant, for skin complaints), which the atlas lists as documented use and nothing more. What laboratory work supports:

  • Both are rich in saponins (reetha pericarp roughly a tenth of its dry weight), and

saponin solutions measurably lower the surface tension of water and lift oily soil: that is a detergent, and the claim "it cleans" is tested.

  • Saponins foam less than synthetic detergents and do not strip oil as hard, which is why

the hair-wash tradition values them.

  • Claims that these washes are "antibacterial" or "cure dandruff" come from traditional

use and some test-tube studies; no clinical trials exist. The atlas does not repeat them as facts.

What is known to be unsafe: saponin water in the eye stings badly; swallowed, it irritates the gut (reetha was used as an emetic for that reason). Neither fruit is eaten.

Soapnut wash water रीठे का पानी

As described in the sources and in common practice:

  • Break ten to fifteen dried reetha shells (seeds removed) into a litre of water. Simmer

twenty to thirty minutes, stirring; the water turns brown and froths. Cool and strain.

  • Use as it is for washing woollens, silk, cotton, and hair; dilute for delicate things.
  • It keeps a few days at room temperature, a week or two in a refrigerator; it is a plant

extract and goes off.

  • For a hair wash, a decoction of equal parts reetha and shikakai, with amla if available,

is the classical form. Keep it out of the eyes.

Neem oil soap: the chemistry and a standard recipe नीम का साबुन

Neem seed oil is bitter, strongly scented, and carries azadirachtin and related compounds that are insecticidal; a neem-oil soap is sold across India for washing skin and livestock and as a garden spray base. In a bar it is usually a fifth or less of the oils, with coconut oil for lather and a bland oil for the body of the bar.

How saponification works. One molecule of triglyceride (a fat) plus three of sodium hydroxide gives one of glycerol and three of sodium soap. Each oil needs a different amount of lye per gram (its saponification value), so the lye is calculated from the exact oils used, and soap-makers deliberately use a little less lye than the calculation (a "superfat" of about five per cent) so that no free alkali is left in the bar.

An example, 1 kg of oils, cold process. Restated from the standard literature; the numbers below are from published saponification values and must be rechecked in a lye calculator for the actual oils before use:

Coconut oil300 g
Neem oil150 g
Rice-bran or groundnut oil550 g
Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), 5 % superfatabout 139 g
Waterabout 330 g
  • Weigh everything; never measure lye by volume. Put on goggles and gloves.
  • Add the lye to the water, outdoors or under a window, stirring; never water to lye.

The solution heats to near boiling and gives off fumes. Let it cool to around 40 °C.

  • Warm the oils to about the same temperature. Pour the lye solution into the oils and

stir (a stick blender takes minutes; a spoon takes an hour) until the mix thickens to a thin custard ("trace").

  • Pour into a lined mould, cover, leave a day or two, then cut into bars.
  • Cure for four to six weeks in air. The reaction finishes and the bar hardens. A

finished bar tests at about pH 9 to 10 on a wetted strip; a bar that stings the tongue or reads above 10 is not finished or has too much lye and must not be used.

Selling it. In India soap sold for the skin is a cosmetic under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act and needs a manufacturing licence from the state authority; a self-help group should check with the district industries centre before selling. Soap for laundry or for garden use is regulated differently. This page does not give legal advice.

Safety, plainly सुरक्षा

  • Sodium hydroxide is the hazard. It blinds on contact with the eye and burns skin

within seconds. Goggles, gloves, long sleeves, no children in the room, a bucket of water and vinegar to hand. Label every container. Store it dry and locked.

  • Neem oil is not to be swallowed. Poisoning of infants given neem oil by mouth is

reported in the Indian medical literature. Keep it, and the soap, away from small children.

  • Saponin washes are safe on skin for most people and painful in the eye. Patch-test

any new wash on the inner arm before using it on the head.

  • No claim on this page is a treatment. A skin condition needs a doctor.

Research direction (a lead, not a result) शोध की दिशा

Sapindus and Senegalia saponins are studied as biosurfactants for textile washing, for cosmetics, and for washing contaminated soils; the work is at laboratory scale. The question for Basti is supply: reetha trees have been disappearing from the plains as nobody planted them, and most shikakai comes from the south. A group that planted reetha on bund lines today would harvest in about ten years.

What we do not know, and how to help हम क्या नहीं जानते

  • Where the nearest reetha trees stand in Basti or Ayodhya, and who collects the fruit.
  • Whether anyone in the district still makes soap, or sells reetha wash, and at what price.
  • Whether shikakai fruits reliably on hedges here.
