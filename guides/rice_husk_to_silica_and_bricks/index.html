धान की भूसी से सिलिका और ईंटRice husk to silica and bricks

Research direction · GDE-0003 · written 2026-10-21

Building material निर्माण सामग्रीResearch leads शोध की दिशा

Every rice mill in the district makes a mountain of husk, and husk ash is mostly silica. What the research says it can become, at what scale that has been shown, and the one hazard that matters.

धान Paddy Oryza sativa गेहूं Wheat Triticum aestivum गन्ना Sugarcane Saccharum officinarum

The material सामग्री

A tonne of Paddy gives about 200 kg of husk at the mill, and 100 kg of husk burnt to ash leaves roughly 18 to 22 kg of ash. That ash is 85 to 95 per cent silica (SiO₂), the rest being potash, lime and unburnt carbon. The plant pulled the silicon out of the soil as silicic acid and laid it down in the husk as a skin of tiny silica bodies; burning removes everything else.

What the ash is like depends entirely on the burn:

  • Burnt below about 700 °C, with air and without hot spots, the silica stays

amorphous: glassy, very porous, chemically reactive. This is the valuable form.

  • Burnt hotter, or left to smoulder for hours in a heap, the silica crystallises into

cristobalite and tridymite. Crystalline ash is nearly useless as a reactive material, and its dust causes silicosis. The uncontrolled husk fires behind rice mills make exactly this.

So the whole subject turns on one piece of equipment: a burner that holds the temperature.

हिंदी: एक टन धान से लगभग 200 किलो भूसी, 100 किलो भूसी से 18–22 किलो राख, और राख में 85–95

Uses, from proven to speculative उपयोग, सिद्ध से संभावित तक

1. Fuel at the mill — in use everywhere

Rice mills across UP already burn husk in their parboiling boilers and some sell power from it. This is the one use at full scale, and it is the reason the ash exists in heaps. The ash from a boiler is usually overburnt and partly crystalline, but it is free.

2. Rice husk ash as a cement substitute — proven in concrete practice

Amorphous husk ash is a pozzolan: mixed with lime or with the lime that cement releases as it sets, it forms the same binding gel as cement itself. The literature on this is forty years deep (Mehta's work from the 1970s onward). Replacing 10 to 30 per cent of the cement in concrete with good husk ash has been shown, in laboratory and field trials, to give equal or better strength and better resistance to water and salts. Husk ash concrete has been used in real structures in India and abroad; the limit is supply of consistent, properly burnt ash, not the chemistry.

For a village builder this translates to: lime-and-husk-ash mortar and plaster, which the Central Building Research Institute at Roorkee worked on decades ago, and

cement-husk-ash blocks cast in ordinary block moulds.

3. Husk ash bricks — shown, not common

Two routes appear in the literature:

  • Fired bricks with husk in the clay. Brick kilns in UP already add husk as a fuel and

pore-former; the bricks come out lighter and insulate better. This is ordinary practice, not an innovation.

  • Unfired lime–husk-ash bricks ("bio-bricks" in some recent papers). Ash, lime and soil

or sand pressed in a block press and cured damp for weeks; the pozzolanic reaction hardens them. Demonstrated by CBRI and in several engineering-college studies; a few small producers exist. Strength is lower than fired brick, enough for single-storey walls, and no coal is burnt. Whether the economics work in Basti against the local kiln price has not been tested.

4. Precipitated silica — shown in factories, not in villages

Amorphous husk ash dissolves in hot caustic soda to give sodium silicate (water glass), and adding acid to that precipitates clean silica. Factories do this; the silica goes into rubber (tyre makers have used husk-derived silica as a filler), toothpaste, paints and insulation. The chemistry is simple; the process involves hot caustic soda and acid and is a small chemical plant, not a cottage industry. The research literature on "nano-silica from rice husk" is large, and almost all of it is laboratory scale.

5. Other things husk and straw become

  • Biochar. Husk charred without air and dug into soil; field trials in India show gains

on acid and sandy soils, little on good alluvium. A lead.

  • Mushroom substrate. Paddy straw, not husk, is the classical bed for

Paddy Straw Mushroom; husk is used in some oyster mushroom mixes.

  • Poultry litter, packing, insulation board. All in use somewhere; none special to here.
  • Silica for the lab. Rice husk ash is a recognised laboratory source of reactive

silica (Chandrasekhar et al. 2003; Sun & Gong 2001 review the chemistry).

What a self-help group could actually try गाँव के पैमाने पर क्या हो सकता है

  1. A controlled husk burner. A brick-lined drum or a two-chamber brick incinerator with

an air inlet, run so the husk burns rather than smoulders and the bed never glows white. Designs exist in the appropriate-technology literature. Target: grey to off-white ash, not black (unburnt carbon) and not pink-white and gritty (overburnt).

  1. Lime–ash mortar for repairs and plaster, the oldest and least risky use, with the ash

sieved and the mix tested on a wall for a season.

  1. Cement–ash blocks with a hand press, starting at 10 per cent replacement and testing

by the simplest means: drop, soak, and load.

Everything beyond that needs a laboratory or a partner institution. The engineering colleges at Gorakhpur and Lucknow, and CBRI Roorkee, are the natural ones to ask.

Safety सुरक्षा

  • Crystalline silica dust causes silicosis, an incurable lung disease. Overburnt or

smouldered husk ash contains it. Anyone handling husk ash in quantity wears a fitted dust mask (not a cloth), wets the ash before shovelling, and does not sieve it indoors.

  • Lime and cement burn skin and eyes; gloves and goggles.
  • The caustic soda and acid of the precipitated-silica route are industrial hazards and

are not for a yard.

हिंदी: सुलगाई हुई भूसी की राख की धूल से सिलिकोसिस होता है, जो लाइलाज है। राख को गीला करके
उठाइए, मास्क पहनिए, घर के अंदर न छानिए। चूना और सीमेंट भी जलाते हैं।

What we do not know, and how to help हम क्या नहीं जानते

  • How many rice mills operate in Basti and Ayodhya districts, how much husk they burn, and

what happens to the ash. A visit to one mill with a notebook would be the first local data point for this page.

  • Whether any local kiln or builder already uses husk ash in mortar.
  • What a local brick costs delivered, which sets the bar any ash block has to clear.
